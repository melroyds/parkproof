# Novel use cases: beyond "me and the sign"
> Part of the ParkProof October 2026 product research. Start with the [README](README.md) and the [synthesis](00-synthesis.md).
>
> Raw output from parallel AI research agents, reproduced verbatim. **Where an individual report and the review disagree, the review wins.** Confidence ratings are the agents' own. Some sources were later found to be unreliable; the review section names them.

**Question.** Family car sharing came from realising the subject is not "me and the sign" but "my car, over time, and everyone who cares about it". What other unlocks hide behind that same assumption?

**Method.** Seven territories researched in parallel. Every use case needed a real source or an explicit NONE FOUND. A ruthless reviewer spot-checked sources live, killed anything unevidenced, fleet-shaped or database-dependent, merged duplicates and ranked the survivors.

## Ruthless review (read this first)

# Ruthless review: ParkProof use-case discovery

Seven territories, roughly 65 proposed use cases, 10 spot-checks fetched live. Verdict up front: **the discovery pass found one very large thing, two real adjacent things, and about forty pieces of padding.** Six of seven territories independently converged on the same use case without noticing, which is the strongest signal in the set and also the clearest evidence that the territory split produced duplication rather than coverage.

### Does not hold up

**RAA "damage while parked" (territory 4's headline evidence for the top-ranked item).** The page says 18% of motor claims, "more than 10,000 claims every year", a table covering 2020-2024, and $250M paid in 2022-23. It does **not** say "2024-25", does **not** say "55,000+ claims", and does **not** say "leading cause in four of the last five financial years". The directional claim survives. Three specific numbers attached to it do not, and they are exactly the kind a hiring PM would check.

**australian5.com, the Shadforth $140 hospital fine (territory 5's only named-victim account).** This is a content farm. Credited to "Editorial Team", no original source, no links to earlier reporting, an "Analysis" section citing unnamed "law enforcement insiders" and "security analysts", the hospital name differs from the report's, and the incident date is given as "March 23" with no year, so "March 2026" is an inference from the publication date. I could not corroborate it in real Australian media because this turn's web search budget was exhausted. **Do not cite this anywhere, and especially not in a case study.** A fabricated or laundered source in a portfolio piece is a credibility event that costs more than the use case is worth.

**INRIX £209 / 45 hours (territory 6's commercial anchor, the "people already pay for parking confidence" argument).** Verified exactly, and dated **12 July 2017**. Nine years old, UK only, presented undated. The behavioural insight is sound. The number is not usable as current Australian willingness-to-pay evidence, and it is the only quantified WTP figure anyone in seven territories found.

**carseal.ai (territory 7's competitive threat).** Real product, and weaker than reported. SHA-256 and Merkle tree confirmed. RFC-3161 is only "may be requested; it is not validated in-app yet". **GPS is not claimed as a feature at all** ("This animation is not a control"). Country unstated, EU-flavoured, rental-only. So ParkProof's KMS chain is genuinely stronger. The actual finding is the price: **$9.99 per report, three for $19.99, no subscription.** That is the only real price anchor anyone produced, and it anchors a one-off purchase, not a subscription.

**Consumer Action on private car parks.** Confirms operators "cannot fine" and that demands rest on alleged breach of contract, and confirms Vico v Care Park ($88 "wholly unexplained"). It does **not mention signage**, and it says "the legal position is unsettled". Territory 7's claim that signage is dispositive is not supported by this source. Worse for the use case: in Victoria people often beat these invoices on the penalty argument or by ignoring them, with no evidence required at all. Narrow it to NSW, where Fair Trading says the contract binds.

**Merri-bek refund scheme.** Date range and the evidence requirement verified. The 248,000 fines and $43-59 overcharge are **not on the council page**, and the scheme **has now ended after 12 months**. The one concrete instance of this use case is closed.

### Could not verify (403 or no URL supplied)

Blocked by the site: drive.com.au (cloned plates, 5,525 cancelled notices), Cairns Council (the one-review rule and the signage-evidence specification, cited across three territories), Kangaroo Island (the 7.5m long-vehicle rule), City of Melbourne tow fees, **Parksy** (301 then 403).

No URL supplied at all, and these are the most load-bearing claims in the entire set:
- **The AFCA Youi determination** (altered photo metadata killed the claim). This is the proof that a signature beats a phone photo. It underwrites the number one ranked use case. No URL. Territory 1 also admits it reached the related policy-exclusion determination "through a search summary" rather than reading it.
- **The AFCA Blue Zebra determination** ($14,856 against a $3,247 offer on the strength of pre-storm photos). No URL.
- **Cato.** Named as a free Australian competitor across all eight jurisdictions. No URL, unverified.

**State it plainly: nobody in seven territories found a single documented instance of a cryptographic signature actually being used, accepted, or even considered in an Australian parking or insurance dispute.** The moat is theoretically clean and empirically untested.

## 2. Kills

Harsh, and the list is long because it should be.

### Killed for "NONE FOUND" or author-admitted absence of evidence

- **Severe weather / hail move-your-car.** Territory 1 proposed it and then dismantled it (BOM licence forbids reuse, Open-Meteo BOM suspended, does not use the evidence chain). Territory 4 independently killed it as "a good-sounding feature with no evidence behind it". Two researchers, same verdict, one of them against his own proposal. Dead.
- **Event-day restrictions.** "I did not find evidence of a documented wave of event-clearway fines or tows." Needs an external fixture feed that never stops growing. Dead.
- **Moving house reserved bays.** The author's own words: "the one I would list and not build". No volume data anywhere. Dead.
- **Plate-keyed digital permits.** "I found no documented case yet of someone fined because their digital permit carried the wrong plate... the harm is inferred rather than observed." Dead as a use case. One line survives inside a vehicle registry.
- **Carer rotation permits.** "No first-hand accounts of carers or households actually being fined." Also asks ParkProof to hold a roster of which plates sit on a council permit, which is council data it cannot hold authoritatively. Dead.
- **Parking someone else's car for someone not present.** The supporting evidence is a Carers Australia snippet that "cut off before the percentage" and a commercial blog. Dead.
- **Fines and tolls for a car you no longer own.** "No Australian data... the volume is unestablished", lowest urgency and lowest WTP by the author's own rating. Dead.
- **Refund-ready archive.** The Merri-bek scheme is verified closed, and the author concedes it is "not a product anyone will buy on its own". Dead as a use case, survives as one sentence justifying why history is stored.
- **Watch me walk back to the car.** Night-safety evidence is real in general, but "no Australian survey isolating car parks specifically". More decisive: a solo developer cannot make a reliability promise about watching someone walk home on top of a service-worker push stack. That is a duty-of-care landmine with zero revenue attached. Dead, and do not revisit.
- **Proving you complied with a policy's parking conditions.** The author's own instruction is "Build the record, not the advice", which means the use case has no independent existence. Folded in.

### Killed as a database in disguise

**Can I sleep here tonight.** Both territories that raised it rated it high-emotion and one rated it hard to build. Neither noticed the real problem. The sign usually says nothing about camping, so the answer comes from a corpus of council by-laws across hundreds of LGAs that ParkProof would have to build and maintain. That is a database requiring critical mass, just maintained by one person instead of a crowd, and it violates the constraint. Add that WikiCamps already owns the audience and the coverage game, and that being confidently wrong at 11pm about "you can sleep here" is the worst possible failure mode in the product. Dead.

### Killed as enforcement against other people

Three territories independently proposed a version of "report the other guy" (blocked driveway, ICE car in an EV bay, accessible bay misuse). Two killed it, the third flagged the tone risk itself. Resolve it permanently: **never.** Snap Send Solve owns the workflow, it needs a council to act, and it converts a product that defends its user into a product that builds cases against neighbours. The cross-territory disagreement resolves against it.

### Killed as fleet, business, or counterparty-dependent

Car-share bay fines (operator owns the notification channel), rental admin fees as the headline (contractual and non-refundable), tow-operator damage claims (no documented pathway to design against), hospital and venue parking as a rates-and-concessions content treadmill, strata enforcement, auto-lodging appeals (one review only, and burning the single shot is harm), permit eligibility screening, digitising the permit itself, a fine guarantee (that is insurance, it needs an AFSL). All correctly dead-ended in the reports. Agreed on all of them, with one addition: the honesty about the AFSL boundary and about the panic-moment unit economics is itself portfolio material. Say it out loud in the case study.

### Demoted rather than killed

- **Long-vehicle 7.5m rule.** Genuinely a new answer, and the corpus is small (one national road rule plus a length test) rather than hundreds of by-laws. But being wrong costs the user $2,200. Tier 3, shipped as a hedged heads-up, never a verdict.
- **EV dual-clock.** Verified correction: Glen Eira has **one** clock, an overstay fee past the posted limit. The second clock, the charging-complete idle fee, belongs to Tesla and Canterbury-Bankstown. The dual-obligation shape is real but it is assembled from two different operators, so the "no app can model this" novelty is thinner than pitched, and WTP is rated low with an affluent, tolerant user base. Tier 3.
- **Trailer and boat-ramp bays.** The winning appeal ground is "no adequate signage", which is the same capability as the missing-sign case. Merged, not standalone. Weekend frequency.
- **Undisplayable motorcycle ticket.** Structurally real, but the existing informal remedy already works (write to the bureau, they usually cancel) and councils are fixing it locally. Folded into the compliance-artefact capture as one extra frame.
- **Visitor permit ledger.** The author's own intensity rating is "low". A scratch-off paper permit cannot be digitised by a third party. Tier 4.

## 3. Merges (this is where the real finding is)

**Merge A. The signed before-photo.** Six of seven territories independently found this. Territory 1's "pre-incident condition record", territory 4's "bracketed absence", territory 6's "the dent that was not there", territory 7's "hit while parked" and "it was already like that", territory 5's "condition at handover", and the evidence half of territory 2's hire-car chargeback. **Six independent researchers, one use case.** That convergence is worth more than any individual piece of evidence in the reports.

**Merge B. The compliance artefact.** Five territories: permit on the dash, paper ticket, pay-by-app confirmation, the plate as typed. One photo covers all four. Territory 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 each wrote it up as separate.

**Merge C. Custody.** Four territories: nomination evidence, who had the car, the handover ledger, nomination under oath. All one thing, and all downstream of family sharing.

**Merge D. The sign as alibi.** Four territories: the missing or changed sign, the tow dispute, the private operator notice, the notice that arrives after the evidence is gone. One capability, proving a negative about signage at a moment in time.

**Merge E. The car is gone.** Three territories: council tow, stolen, private and strata tow. One moment, two variants. The strata variant is the only one that adds something beyond a phone number, because it brings the signage-consent question and the pre-tow condition photo.

**Merge F. Already specced.** I read `docs/ticket-validity-checker.md` (221 lines, "specced, not built"). Territory 4 was right to flag the overlap, and it is wider than flagged. The spec already contains the car-alibi lookup, the contradiction-check concept, the "only ParkProof" moat argument, the decision to store registration with photo-first capture, and the free-one-finding monetisation model. **Territory 7's "car alibi" and "notice after the evidence is gone", and territory 6's clock-start proof, are substantially this spec.** They are not new unlocks. The genuinely new part is that the spec assumes the signed record exists, and today's signed record has no departure half.

### Reasoning on the top ones

**Why #1 wins.** `ended_at` is currently an unsigned localStorage field. Signing the departure is one Lambda route and one KMS call, and it is the missing half of four separate merged use cases at once: duration disputes (the chalk-mark fights), the damage window (the merge-A convergence), the two-minute school zone, and the custody handover. Nothing else in the set converts one day of work into four use cases. The honest caveat is that the top-ranked item rests on an unverified AFCA determination, so the engineering is cheap and the claim needs a real citation before it goes in copy.

**Why #2 is a segment, not a feature.** A verified Australian rider, fined $263, says he now photographs his parking as a precaution. That is a user doing a worse version of ParkProof with his camera roll and telling a journalist about it. Three compounding failure modes (overhang geometry, someone physically moving the bike, undisplayable tickets), daily commuter frequency, densest enforcement in exactly the CBD streets ParkProof already reads, and roughly 982,000 registered motorcycles. **No new product required.** This is the cheapest acquisition insight in the whole pass and the reports buried it as a feature.

**Why #4 is ranked high but at advantage 2.5, not 3.** The permit entitlement engine is the only genuinely new capability found that is neither the evidence chain nor sharing, and the NSW concession table verified exactly, including the counterintuitive result that the ordinary bay often beats the accessible bay. But it is a rules corpus, and a rules corpus is a liability rather than an asset. Territory 3 proposed it and then killed "a standalone national permit rules database" in its own dead-end list. Both are right, and the resolution is the one territory 3 names: inside the photo flow only, with the existing confidence levels and a visible "confirm with your council" escape hatch, never as a lookup authority.

**Why #5 scores well despite rare frequency.** Proving a negative about signage is the one thing a tamper-evident timestamped photo is uniquely good at, and ParkProof already points the camera at the pole as its core gesture. Verified: the Ombudsman's construction-permit-sign case, and the Ombudsman's finding that upholding it was "neither fair nor reasonable". The capture cost is zero because it already happens.

**Why #10 scores lower than its strategic value.** The custody ledger is the back half of the idea Melroy already had, and it is the half with legal weight. Verified: Fines Victoria's reject-and-reissue. It scores 12 only because build cost is 2 and frequency is occasional. Its value is that it changes what family sharing *is*, which the score cannot express.

## 5. Top 5, written out

### 1. Prove the car was fine when you walked away

**Pitch.** Every park you log becomes a signed, GPS-anchored photo of an undamaged car at a known minute, and a signed departure that closes the window, so damage is bounded to a time and a place nobody can argue with.

**Trigger.** You come back and there is a crease in the door and no note under the wiper. Or an insurer asks when it happened. Or a rental desk charges you for a scuff ten days after you flew home.

**Why ParkProof wins.** The app is already standing at the right moment with the camera already open, already geotagged, already signing. Everyone owns after-photos. Almost nobody owns a credible before-photo. The industry's own advice is "take timestamped photos and check the phone's timestamps", which is worth nothing because EXIF is trivially editable and every party in the dispute knows it. Dashcams do not run while parked. carseal.ai sells the artefact standalone at $9.99 and does not claim GPS anchoring.

**Pricing.** **Free capture, paid pack.** The capture has to be habitual or the archive never exists, so charging for it kills the asset. Sell the export: the bounded-window dispute pack with the signed exhibits, the verification walkthrough, and the narrow-window CCTV request letter that tells the user they have roughly fourteen days before the footage is overwritten. A one-off purchase at the carseal anchor is also defensible for a single incident.

### 2. Prove the permit, the ticket, or the payment was in place

**Pitch.** One extra frame at park time, inside the same signed record, proving the permit was on the dash, the ticket was on the windscreen, and this is the plate you actually typed.

**Trigger.** A fine arrives saying you did not display a permit you did display, or saying nobody paid for your vehicle when you paid for a vehicle one character away from yours.

**Why ParkProof wins.** Councils have published the specification: date, time, location, plate, and a copy of the valid permit. ParkProof manufactures exactly that, contemporaneously, which is the one thing nobody can stage after the notice arrives. Verified: the Ombudsman's 1,200 PayStay drivers, the finding that the council "could also have verified" the payments while refusing to, and the Mary case where a valid permit blew off and the council refused until the Ombudsman called. The one-review-only rule makes a complete bundle worth more than the sum of its parts, because there is no second attempt.

**Pricing.** **Free.** This is one frame in a flow that must become a habit, and the cohort is disability permit holders facing $581 to $697 fines plus a demerit point. Charging them for the correct reading is indefensible and it is also the best acquisition story the product will ever have. Monetise the appeal built on top.

### 3. The handover ledger, and the rail that stops sharing from hurting people

**Pitch.** A shared car keeps a signed record of who had it and when, which is the record the law already assumes you keep, and the same record suppresses the permit concession when the permit holder is not aboard.

**Trigger.** A notice arrives three weeks late for an afternoon you were at work, and you must nominate a driver under penalty of a false declaration with full name, address, date of birth and licence number, before the enforcement order issues.

**Why ParkProof wins.** Verified: Fines Victoria says the nominee can reject the nomination and the fine is reissued to you, and councils state that failing to keep a record of who had the car is not an adequate excuse. Nothing a household owns produces that record. Nothing else knows both the sign and the roster.

**The rail is not optional.** Verified: ACT's own parking director says most permit misuse is family members using the permit when the holder is not in the car, which is $600 in the ACT and grounds for confiscation and revocation in NSW. **A shared-car feature that silently carries the permit concession across drivers is a $600 liability generator, and the person who loses the permit is the one who depends on it.** Build the handover prompt that asks whether the permit holder is travelling, or do not build sharing.

**Pricing.** **Paid.** Household features are where consumers accept a subscription, and this is the household feature.

### 4. Tell me what MY permit gets me, at THIS sign, in THIS council

**Pitch.** The sign says 30 minutes. Your NSW permit makes it two hours in the ordinary bay and exactly 30 minutes in the accessible bay beside it, and nobody has ever told you that.

**Trigger.** A permit holder pulls up to a timed sign outside a medical centre with a 50-minute appointment.

**Why ParkProof wins.** Coordinates already resolve the timezone, so they already resolve the council and the state. One stored fact (permit type and issuing state) changes the answer. The entitlement layer is pure published-rules reasoning with no vision call, which is the cheap synchronous path refresh mode already proved. Nothing on the market does this. The NSW government's own Park'nPay finds empty bays with in-ground sensors and interprets no entitlements at all.

**Pricing.** **Free**, with a hard constraint. Ship it inside the photo flow with the existing confidence levels and a visible council escape hatch, exactly the pattern already used for stacked signs. Any engine that defaults to "double it" will confidently tell users to overstay in most of the country.

### 5. Prove what the sign said, or that it was not there

**Pitch.** A signed, GPS-stamped wide shot of the pole as it stood when you parked, which is the one piece of evidence councils ask for and almost nobody can produce afterwards.

**Trigger.** A works crew bolts a temporary sign to the pole overnight. Or a tow-away zone you are certain did not say what they say it said. Or a private operator's invoice turns on signage you cannot remember.

**Why ParkProof wins.** This is the purest case for tamper-evidence, because it requires proving a negative. Forum consensus is that the photo only counts if it predates the challenge, since the council can trim the tree or replace the sign afterwards. Verified: the Ombudsman found upholding the construction-sign fine "neither fair nor reasonable", and the council's own position is that conditions take effect as soon as signs are visible, with no notice owed.

**Narrow the private-operator half.** Consumer Action says the legal position is unsettled and does not mention signage, and Vico turned on the amount being an unexplained penalty, not on signage. In Victoria people often win without any evidence. Scope the private-operator framing to NSW, where Fair Trading says the entry terms bind.

**Pricing.** **Free capture, paid pack.** Same shape as #1.

## 6. The reframes

Family sharing was a reframe because it changed the subject from "me and the sign" to "my car and everyone who cares about it". These are the ones at that altitude, not feature-sized.

**Reframe 1. The car has a custody timeline, and the owner is liable along all of it.** Australian law already assumes you keep a contemporaneous record of who had your car, and nobody does. This is the strongest find because it inverts the value of family sharing. Sharing looks like convenience and is actually record-keeping, which means the feature's real output is not visibility but an artefact with legal weight. Verified by primary source.

**Reframe 2. Share the session, not the person.** This is the most valuable strategic find in all seven reports and it is a positioning moat no amount of engineering buys. Verified Australian parents call continuous tracking "creepy/stalkery" and connect it to coercive control, a moderator objects to normalising it, and the same parents happily accept arrival confirmations and pickup logistics. A parking session is a bounded object with a start, an address, an expiry and an explicit end. Life360 structurally cannot offer that, because its unit is a person's location feed. ParkProof can, and it is consistent with the anonymous-by-default posture that is currently the product's credibility. Build the teen as a first-class participant who sees exactly what the parent sees, because the evidence says consent is the whole game.

**Reframe 3. Every park is a signed before-photo.** Today the evidence chain is a dispute-only feature, so it pays off on roughly one park in two hundred. If every session captures a signed condition baseline, the chain pays off on every park, and the subscription story changes from "insurance against a rare fine" to "a record that accrues". That is also the only credible answer to the frequency problem that sinks almost everything else on the list.

**Reframe 4. The entitlement is attached to a person, not the car.** For roughly a million Australians the sign is only half the input. This breaks the one-user-one-car assumption from a completely different direction than sharing does, and it is the only reframe here that produces a new answer rather than a new artefact.

**The boundary that matters more than any of them.** Territory 7's closing dead end is the single most important sentence in all seven reports: do not reposition as a "vehicle evidence platform". Every use case above works because the app is already open at the moment of parking for a reason the user already cares about. The parking job is the distribution mechanism for the evidence chain. Repositioning loses the trigger and turns a weekly product into one people remember only after something has already gone wrong, which is exactly the failure every use case here describes. Hold the line.

## 7. What I did not find

**No evidence that the moat has ever been exercised.** Zero documented instances, across seven territories, of a cryptographic signature being used, accepted, or even considered in an Australian parking or insurance dispute. The two AFCA determinations that would establish it come with no URLs, and one was reached "through a search summary" rather than read. **The number one ranked use case rests on an unverified citation.** Get one real determination URL or strip the insurance framing out of all public copy. The defensible claim is narrower and still strong: the signature removes the other side's ability to allege your photos were tampered with. Claiming more will not survive a hiring PM who knows insurance.

**No verified competitive read.** Parksy returned 403 and Cato came with no URL, so the "appeal letters are already free and commoditised" conclusion, which drives a major strategy call, is unverified. carseal.ai verified and turned out weaker than reported. Resolve the tension the reports leave hanging: the letter is table stakes, the signed exhibit is the moat, and both propositions are correct.

**No Australian willingness-to-pay data at all.** The only quantified figure is INRIX at £209, which is UK and 2017. The only real price signal is carseal's $9.99 one-off. Nobody found an Australian number. That is the biggest hole in the pass, because it is the question the monetisation decision actually turns on.

**Frequency is the unsolved problem.** Almost every survivor is "occasional" or "rare", which is a poor subscription shape and every territory noticed separately without any of them naming the consequence. Only four things are plausibly weekly for the same person: the school-run countdown, the motorcycle commuter, the EV apartment dweller, and the permit holder. Three of those four are small populations. That is the retention story, and it is thin.

**One untested behavioural assumption underneath the whole ranking.** Every top use case assumes people will take a second photo, or a guided multi-angle capture, or a dashboard frame. Nobody researched whether they will. ParkProof's own telemetry can answer it this week.

**Genuinely empty idea space.** Anything requiring a counterparty to cooperate. Seven territories, seven independent dead-ends on the same wall: councils, insurers, charger operators, rental companies, strata, police. The constraint is not a limitation on the research, it is the finding.

**Internal inconsistencies to clean before anything is published.** NSW Health hospital parking revenue is cited as $51.69M (one territory) and "more than $87 million in 2024-25" (another). AAMI appears as "more than 22,000 car park claims Oct 2023 to Sep 2024" and "more than 17,300 Sept 2024 to Aug 2025". Both pairs are probably different releases. Using both in one case study looks sloppy.

## 8. Next move

**Two things, in this order.**

1. **Read the two numbers you already have before building anything.** What fraction of sessions include the optional car photo today, and what fraction get explicitly ended with "I've left"? The Layer 1 and Layer 2 feedback pipeline already exists. Those two numbers decide whether the entire top of this ranking is buildable behaviour or wishful thinking, and they cost a CloudWatch Logs Insights query.

2. **Sign `ended_at`.** One route, one KMS call, no new infrastructure. It is the keystone of four merged use cases and it is the cheapest high-value change in the whole set. Everything else in the top five is a capture prompt and a PDF layout on top of it.

Then write the family sharing spec with the permit-suppression rail in it from the first draft, not as a follow-up. Sharing without that rail is worse than no sharing.

### Territory 1: When something happens TO the parked car: towing, theft, damage while parked, vandalism, and the insurance / dispute evidence problem that follows

The reframe is bigger than family sharing. Every use case in this territory is the same question in different clothes: CUSTODY. Who had the car, where exactly was it, in what condition, from when to when, and who says so. ParkProof already captures all five and signs them. The strongest finding is that the proof problem is real and expensive: AAMI logged 17,300+ shopping-centre car-park claims in one year with 40% being accidental damage to parked vehicles, an NRMA survey found more than two in three Sydney drivers had their parked car damaged and 91% of drivers admit driving off without leaving a note, and the canonical failure pattern is documented in forum after forum — you return to damage, you cannot say when it happened, the centre refuses CCTV, the police report assigns no fault, and you pay the excess. The second strongest finding is the panic moment: a Melbourne clearway tow costs $504.70 release plus a $209 fine as at 1 July 2026, rising to $984.68 after 14 days, and the towed-or-stolen triage is genuinely fragmented (Nationwide on 13 48 69 for most of Melbourne, separate arrangements for City of Melbourne and Port Phillip), with forum consensus that step one is to call the number on the sign you can no longer see. ##### Who actually had the car (nomination evidence)

- **Situation.** A parking fine arrives in your name for a Saturday afternoon. Your adult son had the car. He says it wasn't him, or he says he will pay it and then doesn't, or he genuinely doesn't remember. ##### Severe weather heading for the car you left outside

- **Situation.** Your car is on a side street in Northcote. A supercell is forty minutes out with large hail. Every insurer's advice is the same, get it under cover or cover it with blankets, and act early. Nobody tells you, because nothing knows where your car is right now.

#### Dead ends

- Vandalism and keying as a standalone use case. It looks like an obvious fit and it is not. The offender is essentially never identified, insurers treat the claim as at-fault because there is nobody to recover from, and Australian excesses commonly cited at $600-1,000 often exceed the repair cost, so people pay privately and never claim. A Melbourne panel shop puts it plainly: a claim usually costs more than paying cash for a single key line. A signed photograph proves a fact nobody is disputing. The evidence does not change the outcome, so the feature does not change anything.
- Council liability for a falling tree branch. Tempting because ParkProof knows the car was under that tree, undamaged, at 9am. But the decisive question is not whether your car was there, it is whether the council had prior knowledge of a specific risk and failed to act. Bendigo council refused a branch claim because there had been no earlier complaints about the tree, Kempsey council states that trees will from time to time lose branches and that it requires prior knowledge of any specific risk, and Bryant v ACT (2022, ACAT) dismissed a claim because the risk of the branch falling was not foreseeable. ParkProof's evidence sits on the wrong axis entirely.
- Shopping trolley damage as its own product. The decisive questions are which retailer owned the trolley and whether anyone was negligent, and the answer lives in CCTV. OzBargain threads on exactly this at Chadstone show people cannot even establish whether the trolley was Coles' or Woolworths'. There is nothing here that the narrow-window CCTV request does not already cover, and dressing it up separately pads the list.
- Any crowd-sourced layer: a map of where cars get towed, a feed of reported tows, shared hazard reports, a community register of dodgy signs. Explicitly excluded by the constraints and correctly so, since all of them are worthless until they have density and a solo developer cannot buy density.
- Flood alerts for a parked car. Same mechanism as hail with a much thinner evidence base, and the advice is actively complicated because moving a car that has already been in floodwater is dangerous and can cause further damage. If the weather-warning layer gets built, hail is the use case, not flood.
- Blocked driveway reporting. This is enforcement against somebody else's car, not something happening to yours, and Snap Send Solve already owns the workflow and collects exactly what councils ask for: rego, make, model, colour, date, time, place and photos. Do not re-fight a battle that is already lost to a product people have installed.
- Building a flow around claiming against a tow operator for damage done during the tow. The before-and-after record genuinely helps, but I could find no Australian source on how these claims are decided, which tribunal hears them, or whether operator liability is capped. Australian guidance says to pay the fees first and dispute afterwards, and NSW towing invoice reviews take at least twelve weeks. Without a documented pathway there is nothing to design a flow against. Let the condition record cover it incidentally.
- Marketing any of this on the premise that insurers will accept a signed evidence pack. The industry's 2026 answer to the authenticity problem is insurer-initiated and B2B: Truepic inside Verisk's ClaimSearch, where an adjuster requests verified capture when a claim is flagged. There is no evidence any Australian insurer accepts a consumer-supplied C2PA or signed-photo artefact as a recognised class of evidence. The defensible claim is narrower and still strong: the signature removes the insurer's ability to allege your photos were tampered with, which is precisely what killed the claimant in the Youi determination. Claiming more than that will not survive contact with a hiring PM who knows insurance.
- Competing with Find My and Google's parked-car detection on 'where is my car'. Apple and Google own device-based car location and ship it free on every phone. ParkProof should never position itself as telemetry. Its claim is narrower and unassailable: not where the car is, but what was recorded about it when you parked, signed so nobody can say you made it up later.
- Monetising the panic moment itself. Every one of these events is rare per person and the willingness to pay spikes precisely when the money is already spent, at the impound counter or after the excess is charged. A pay-at-the-moment-of-crisis product is a terrible business and an obviously terrible one, which matters because a hiring PM will ask. The only honest monetisation shape here is a cheap annual peace-of-mind layer sold on the back of a story about the panic moment, and the discipline of saying that out loud in the case study is worth more than pretending the unit economics are good.

### Territory 2: Vehicles that are not an ordinary private car: motorcycles and scooters, EVs, caravans/trailers/boats, utes and towing rigs, hire cars, and car-share vehicles. Australian rules, real confusion, and where ParkProof's signed evidence chain has an unfair advantage.

The territory is richer than expected, but not for the reason the brief implies. The pattern across every non-standard vehicle class is that the fine does not turn on what the sign said. It turns on a fact about the vehicle at a moment: was it plugged in, was it wholly inside the line, was a trailer attached, was it over 7.5 metres, was someone sleeping in it, was it me driving. ParkProof's sign reader answers the sign question. Its KMS-signed timestamped photo answers the vehicle-state question, and that second asset is currently doing almost nothing. For non-ordinary vehicles it is the more valuable of the two. Motorcycles and scooters generate the most fines by a wide margin because they have three independent, compounding failure modes, and riders are already manually doing a worse version of ParkProof: one Sydney rider interviewed by webBikeWorld "now photographs his parking as a precaution" after someone dragged his bike out of a bay. That is the strongest demand signal in the whole research set. The genuinely unclear rules sit with caravans, trailers and boats, where councils contradict each other in print about whether the limit is one hour, no limit, 28 days or three days, and with sleeping in a vehicle, where a state road authority reportedly concedes it has no legislation covering rest-area overnight stays. Hire cars are the highest-emotion, highest-dollar event: the fine lands four to eight weeks later through the rental company with a non-refundable $49 to $55 admin fee attached, the renter often cannot even see the notice, and the Victorian Ombudsman has already documented 1,200-plus Melbourne motorists fined $83 each for a single mistyped registration character, which is exactly the error a driver who has never seen the plate before is most likely to make. EVs are the structurally most interesting because they are the only class where the obligation is conditional (be actively charging AND inside the posted limit) rather than a time window, which forces a new data model rather than a new screen. Car-share is the weakest vein in the territory and I would not build for it. Note: my web search budget ran out before I could verify statutory time limits for serving a parking infringement in NSW and Victoria, which matters for how long evidence needs to be retained.

#### Use cases

##### Proof of how I left it (the moved-bike and overhang defence)

- **Situation.** You park your scooter nose-in to a motorcycle bay in the CBD at 8:10am, wholly inside the line, and walk to work. At some point another rider lifts the back of your bike and shuffles it forward to squeeze theirs in, or you simply misjudged the line by six centimetres because your top box sticks out further than your wheels do. At 4:20pm an officer photographs the bike partly outside the bay and issues a no-stopping or not-completely-within-a-bay notice. You appeal. The reviewing authority looks at the officer's photo, which is the only photo that exists, and upholds it. You get one review application in Victoria, so there is no second attempt.
- **Who feels it.** Commuting motorcycle and scooter riders in Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane and Adelaide CBDs. Roughly 982,000 registered motorcycles nationally, concentrated in exactly the inner-city streets where enforcement is densest and bays are smallest.

##### My rig is nine metres today (the long-vehicle rule nobody knows about)

- **Situation.** You hitch the boat or the van to the ute on Friday night and park the whole rig in the street outside your house or near the ramp. The ute alone is five metres and legal anywhere. The combination is over 7.5 metres, which makes it a long vehicle, which in a built-up area means one hour, day or night, unless loading or unloading. You had no idea the rule existed, it is not on the sign, and in South Australia you can collect a fresh expiation notice for every further hour the rig stays there.
- **Who feels it.** Anyone who tows: around 863,000 registered caravans in Australia as at January 2026 and over 817,000 towable RVs in the wider fleet, plus boat and trailer owners. The person caught is typically a homeowner who believes, correctly for their car and incorrectly for their rig, that the street outside their house is unrestricted.
- **Evidence.** Australian Road Rule 200 restricts a heavy or long vehicle from stopping on a road in a built-up area for more than one hour unless signage or another law permits it. Councils publish that the length test is the combination: one states it 'includes any vehicle with a trailer or similar attached to the vehicle, which together exceed 7.5 metres'. Kangaroo Island Council states that in unrestricted parking areas a long vehicle over 7.5m 'can be parked for no longer than one hour, day or night, unless loading or unloading is taking place'. West Torrens' fact sheet confirms South Australian drivers may incur an expiation notice plus further notices for each hour the vehicle remains. Brisbane City Council's local law additionally restricts such vehicles within 100 metres of residential land. Penrith notes a maximum penalty of $2,200 for illegal parking in residential areas. Councils also contradict each other on duration generally: Sunshine Coast says there is no time limit for a caravan, boat or trailer absent a signed limit, Wollongong staff said there is no time limit but rangers can fine oversized vehicles, NSW applies a 28-day unattended rule for registered and 15 days (or three days per one council) for unregistered, and City of Charles Sturt said a seven-metre four-tonne caravan is 'well within its rights to be there'. Enforcement is real and rising: Randwick rangers investigated 932 suspected unattended vehicles in 2025, impounded 440 and issued over $28,000 in fines, and Sutherland Shire received over 2,400 complaints about boats and trailers plus over 500 about caravans in 2025 before launching a six-month $140 fine trial from late July 2026.
- **Source.** <https://www.kangarooisland.sa.gov.au/recreation/expecttheunexpected/parking>
- **Why ParkProof.** This is the clearest case for giving ParkProof a vehicle profile with a towing toggle, because it changes the correct answer to the same sign in the same spot. No other consumer parking product knows or cares what you are towing. The sign reader already resolves timezone from coordinates and already does multi-rule leave-by maths, picking the earliest constraint. Adding an unsigned statutory constraint (you are a long vehicle, so one hour applies even though the sign says nothing) is the same computation with one more rule injected from the vehicle profile rather than from the image. That is a genuinely new answer no competitor gives, and it demonstrates that the product models the user's world rather than the photograph, which is the kind of thing a hiring PM notices. The honest caveat is that correctness across eight jurisdictions plus local laws is the hard part, and being confidently wrong here costs a user $2,200, so this should ship as a heads-up with a 'check your council' hedge rather than a verdict.

##### Charging is not parking (the dual-condition EV bay)

- **Situation.** You plug in at a council charger at 10am for a two-hour limit. Charging finishes at 11:20am. At 12:05pm you are still plugged in because you are in a meeting, and you are now being billed a dollar a minute in idle fees, or ticketed for exceeding the posted limit, or both. Alternatively you arrive, the charger is faulty, you park in the bay anyway because you intend to charge, and you are now an EV parked in a charging bay without actively charging, which is its own offence. Nobody can tell you which of those two obligations you are breaching at any given minute, because they run on different clocks.
- **Who feels it.** Australia's EV fleet passed 612,000 vehicles by the end of June 2026, with EVs taking around half of new car sales in the record second quarter. Most acute for apartment dwellers with no home charger, who use public chargers several times a week and are therefore exposed to this rule shape constantly rather than occasionally.
- **Evidence.** Glen Eira City Council announced that from 1 October 2026 its public chargers operate as a paid service with $0.25 per kWh plus $1.00 per minute once a vehicle passes the advertised time limit. Canterbury-Bankstown Council charges $1.00 per minute of overstay from 1 July 2026 after a two-hour limit with a ten-minute grace, and warns 'you could also be fined by a parking inspector for exceeding the 2-hour limit', which is the double-jeopardy shape. City of Newcastle states that parking time limits apply to EV charging bays 'irrespective of whether your charging session is complete'. City of Adelaide monitors on-street EV bays against the signed time limit and can expiate overstays. Tesla charges 50 cents per minute for remaining plugged in after charging completes. On the other side, NSW Road Rule 203C and equivalents in Queensland, Tasmania and the NT require an EV in a charging bay to be in the process of charging, with NSW penalties up to $2,200; South Australia fines an EV parked in a charging bay without connecting $111, versus $75 for an ICE car in the same bay. I found NO evidence either way on whether a faulty charger is a defence, and no Australian forum thread about an EV driver fined at a broken charger, so that specific pain is unverified.
- **Source.** <https://www.gleneira.vic.gov.au/about-council/news/latest-news/changes-to-help-keep-ev-chargers-available>
- **Why ParkProof.** Every parking app in existence models a single expiry time. This bay has two independent obligations with different clocks and different consequences, and ParkProof's data model is the only one in reach that could represent it, because it already derives active sessions from multiple states rather than one timer, and already pushes background web notifications via VAPID and EventBridge rather than relying on an open tab. The right product is a two-clock session: a charging-complete clock driving a 'come unplug me before the idle fee starts' push, and a posted-limit clock driving the ordinary expiry push, with the earlier one winning. No charger API or operator partnership is needed, which matters given the no-partnerships constraint: the user photographs the charger screen and the bay sign, and the vision pipeline reads the session state and the limit off both. The evidence chain earns its place on the other side of the coin, for the faulty-charger and the I-was-plugged-in disputes, where a signed photo of the plug in the socket and the charger's error screen at a known minute is the only proof that exists. Honest read: this is the most structurally novel use case and the one with the weakest willingness to pay, because the losses are small and the users are affluent and tolerant.

##### Trailer bays and car bays look identical when you are backing a boat down a ramp

- **Situation.** You arrive at the boat ramp at 5am, launch, and park. The bays near the ramp are reserved for vehicles with trailers and the ones you want are not, or the reverse, and the only sign saying so is a single one at the far end of a large unlit gravel area. One fined driver said she appealed on the basis that there was no signage and had been successful on an earlier fine. At Safety Beach in Victoria a resident fined $85 said he had not known the area was off limits to vehicles not towing, arguing there was only one no-parking sign in the whole car-and-trailer area, while the shire confirmed the bays were reserved but said officers may use discretion based on occupancy. At Port Douglas locals complained in 2024 that 'people have been getting tickets even if their wheel is hanging over the line by a small margin', with the council conceding that the size of modern cars and boats often results in vehicles overhanging the designated spaces and that it was reviewing layout and signage. ##### Can I sleep here tonight (the overnight question, which is not the parking question)

- **Situation.** It is 10:40pm, you are in a van or a caravan on the edge of a coastal town, and you need to know not whether you can park for two hours but whether you can still be here, asleep, at 6am. The sign says No Camping, which in NSW rest areas reportedly means 'no longer than overnight' rather than 'no overnight'. The council one street over bans it outright. The state has no law against it. Nobody can tell you, and the cost of guessing wrong is being woken at 2am and told to move, or a fine, while the cost of not guessing is driving on tired in the dark.
- **Who feels it.** Caravan and motorhome travellers (around 937,000 registered RVs in Australia; 17.3 million caravan and camping trips in 2025 generating 57.9 million visitor nights), van-dwellers, backpackers in campervans, and people sleeping in a car for fatigue management.
- **Evidence.** A free-camping directory reports that NSW Roads and Maritime Services confirmed it 'currently has no legislation relating to the use of Rest Areas for overnight stays', while warning such use 'may be deemed illegal by them and subject to localised interpretation by authorities', and interprets 'No Camping' signs at those rest areas as meaning you may not stay longer than overnight. Brisbane City Council says it is illegal to camp overnight in a vehicle on a road in Brisbane, with named exceptions for fatigue management, personal safety, emergencies, designated signed areas and caravan parks. RAA says there are no South Australian state laws against sleeping in your car but most SA councils have by-laws preventing overnight sleeping in parks and car parks, and that even without signs you could be told to move on or fined. CarExpert says Victoria has no law making it illegal but some councils may. Two commercial sources flatly contradict each other on Melbourne, one saying sleeping in any vehicle including campervans is prohibited and the other that Victoria is legal at state level with councils attempting by-laws. The Shire of Wyndham East Kimberley permits up to 24 consecutive hours only in a designated 24-hour rest area. A 2017 'No Camping' sign on the Pacific Highway near Ballina became a flashpoint precisely because nobody agreed what it meant.
- **Source.** <https://www.thegreynomads.com.au/parking-4/>
- **Why ParkProof.** This is the clearest example in the territory of a question the sign alone cannot answer, which is exactly where an AI sign reader plus jurisdiction context beats a photograph of a sign. The existing pipeline already resolves coordinates to a timezone and reasons about multi-rule time windows, which is the right machinery for 'this restriction starts at midnight and that one lifts at 7am, so the binding constraint is X'. The signed evidence chain has a secondary role for the ranger encounter: a timestamped record of arrival time supports a fatigue-management defence in the jurisdictions that recognise one. I am rating this hard to build, not easy, and I would argue against shipping it as a verdict. The rules are genuinely contradictory in the source material, a state road authority says there is no legislation, and being confidently wrong about 'you can sleep here' at 11pm is a materially worse failure than being wrong about a two-hour bay. If built at all, build it as a disclosed-uncertainty briefing (here is what the sign says, here is the state rule, here is this council's by-law if known, here is the confidence) rather than a yes or no. That restraint is itself the interesting product decision.
- **Scores.** emotional intensity high · frequency daily · willingness to pay medium · build hard

#### Dead ends

- Car-share wrong-bay and occupied-pod fines (GoGet, Flexicar, Uber Carshare). Looks attractive because the audience is concentrated (GoGet alone reports 200,000 to 280,000 members, around 170,000 in NSW) and the pain is specific. It does not survive. The evidence is one unverified Trustpilot account of a $49 charge where support could not point to the authorising clause, and the strongest supporting case is Zipcar Flex in the UK (over GBP600 in fines, GBP15 per fine processing), not Australia. More fatally, the operator controls the whole loop: the fine arrives through the operator's app, the operator decides whether to nominate you, and the operator can close the gap any time by improving its own in-app bay guidance, which it has every incentive to do. Building consumer evidence tooling against a counterparty who owns the notification channel is a bad position. The adjacent framing (helping the operator) is explicitly out of scope as B2B.
- Bike-rack and towball obscured number plate ($385 in Victoria, up to $514 and three demerits in NSW, and it applies even with no bikes loaded). Real, well documented and genuinely surprising to people. But it is a roadworthiness and driving offence detected while the vehicle is moving, not a parking decision, and ParkProof has no advantage: there is no sign to read, no time window to compute, and no contested fact that a signed photo settles. The honest product here is a checklist, which is a blog post, not a feature.
- Motorcycle ANPR failure in private car parks (no front plate, so plate-recognition boom gates and ticketless systems cannot read a bike). Mechanically interesting and confirmed as a problem in principle: a Queensland parliamentary submission notes motorcycles carry only a rear plate so 'the current arrangement is not suitable for their identification', and Canterbury riders in the UK had to ride away from three ANPR car parks. But I found zero Australian consumer accounts of a rider receiving a breach notice from this, and the failure mode is usually that the rider cannot enter or cannot be charged, which is a windfall rather than a grievance. No pain, no product.
- A crowd-sourced map of motorcycle bays, EV chargers with real time limits, or trailer-friendly parking. This is the obvious-looking answer to 'the rules vary by council' and it is ruled out twice over: it needs critical mass to be useful, which a solo developer cannot bootstrap, and the incumbent directories (PlugShare for chargers, WikiCamps for overnight stays) already hold that network. Competing on coverage against an established database is the one fight ParkProof cannot win. Reading the sign in front of you is the whole point of the product precisely because it works with one user.
- Reporting an ICE car blocking your charging bay, or a car in a trailer bay. Emotionally satisfying, genuinely a daily irritation, and the state penalties are eye-watering ($2,200 in NSW, up to $3,200 in the ACT, $2,875 in Queensland). But the report has to reach a council or charger operator to produce any outcome, and council partnerships are explicitly unavailable, so the feature terminates in a dead end and the user learns their evidence went nowhere. Worse, it points the product at other people's behaviour rather than the user's own exposure, which is a different and uglier product. The EV driver's own dual-obligation fine risk is the larger and more serviceable problem.
- Caravan street-storage disputes with councils and neighbours (the 28-day rules, impoundment, the Randwick and Sutherland enforcement waves). The rules here are the most genuinely unclear in the entire territory, which is why it is tempting. But the person with the burning need is the complaining neighbour, not the owner, and the owner's defence is a dated log proving the van moved inside the window, which is a once-a-month logging chore with no moment of anxiety attached to it. Low frequency, no trigger, and the real fix is local-law reform, which Sutherland Shire is already lobbying the Minister for. Not a consumer product.
- A pre-trip 'will my vehicle fit this bay' estimator. Modern bays really are too small (a HiLux dual cab at about 5.27 metres in a 5.4 metre bay leaves roughly 15 centimetres, and Standards Australia is reportedly moving off-street bays to 5.6 metres), and overhang fines are real. But estimating bay length from a photo is unreliable, and more importantly the driver already knows their ute is enormous. The fine is for where they stopped, not for a fact they could have looked up. The useful product is evidence of how they actually parked, which the first use case above already covers.
- Chasing rental-company admin fees as the headline problem rather than the fine. The $49 to $55 fee is the part that enrages people and the part ParkProof can least affect: it is contractual, disclosed in the rental agreement, charged for the act of nominating you, and typically not refunded even when the underlying fine is withdrawn. Positioning as 'beat the admin fee' would be a promise the product cannot keep. The defensible promise is narrower and truer: be able to reconstruct and prove where that car was, so the fine itself can be fought and a chargeback has evidence behind it.

### Territory 3: Permits and entitlements: where the sign is not the whole story

The reframe holds, and it goes further than family sharing. ParkProof reads the sign, but for roughly a million Australians the sign is only half the input: the other half is a permit whose entitlement is attached to a PERSON, varies by STATE, and in Western Australia varies by COUNCIL. I built the comparison from primary sources and the regimes genuinely contradict each other. A 30-minute sign becomes 2 hours in NSW and the ACT, double time in Victoria, and free-no-limit in Queensland. More counterintuitively, in Victoria, NSW, SA and NT the accessible bay gives you LESS time than the ordinary bay beside it, because concessions do not apply inside the bay, so the rational move is often not to use it. Nobody tells people this. The highest-value finding for the car-sharing reframe is that the ACT's own parking director said most permit "fraud" is family members using the permit when the holder is not in the car, a $600 offence in the ACT and grounds for confiscation and revocation in NSW. That is good-faith families committing an offence that costs their parent the permit they depend on, and it is the exact failure mode the family-sharing model creates. Three structural shifts make this urgent rather than merely true: permits are going plate-keyed and invisible (Northern Beaches moved 208,000 permits off windscreens in 2026, Brimbank states permits are no longer displayed on the vehicle), enforcement is going automated (Brisbane: 176,494 fines, $34.9M in nine months), and the Victorian Ombudsman has documented that councils resolve the resulting disputes with "a mindset that the customer is usually wrong." The asymmetry is the opportunity: the council has a timestamped photo of your plate and you have nothing. ParkProof already manufactures the missing artefact. Honest caveat: I could not verify the VicRoads FAQ directly (403) and leaned on council pages for the Victorian blue/green split, and the carer and bay-misuse items are under-evidenced because the search budget ran out.

#### Use cases

##### The permit-aware answer: what MY permit gets me, at THIS sign, in THIS council

- **Situation.** A Sydney woman with an MPS permit pulls up to a 30-minute sign outside a medical centre. She has a 50-minute appointment. She believes she gets double time, because that is what her Melbourne sister told her. She is wrong in both directions: in NSW a 30-minute sign actually becomes two full hours, so she had plenty of time and did not need to rush out mid-consult. Had the sign said 15 minutes she would have had only 30 minutes, not 30 doubled. And had she taken the accessible bay next to it with a posted time limit, she would have had no concession at all and been over at 30 minutes. Same woman, same permit, three different answers depending on a rule she has never been shown, and the fine for getting it wrong is $581 to $697 plus a demerit point.
- **Who feels it.** Australian Disability Parking Scheme permit holders and the family members who drive them. Roughly 5.5 million Australians report disability (21.4% of the population) and about 556,000 always need assistance with mobility away from home, so the permit-holding population is plausibly in the high hundreds of thousands. ##### The borrowed-car permit trap: the entitlement does not travel with the car

- **Situation.** Mum's permit lives in Mum's car. Saturday morning her son borrows the car to do her shopping, because that is the entire point of the arrangement. He parks in an accessible bay at the shops, or claims the free-meter concession, because the permit is sitting right there on the dash and he is literally running her errands. In the ACT that is a $600 fine. In NSW an authorised officer can confiscate the permit on the spot and Transport for NSW can revoke it outright. In Tasmania it is still an offence even if the permit holder stays in the car while someone else runs the errand. Family members may use the permit, but only while actually transporting the holder, and essentially nobody knows the line sits there.
- **Who feels it.** Multi-driver households where one member holds the permit. Adult children of ageing parents, partners of permit holders, and the permit holder themselves, who bears the real loss because the permit is attached to them and it is their independence that gets revoked, not the driver's licence.
- **Evidence.** The ACT's Parking Operations Director Chris Seddon, announcing 579 infringements in four months (272 from 15 February to 15 March 2019, plus 307 more to 15 June), said directly: 'Most of the fraudulent use is due to people, other than the permit holder, using the permit when the permit holder is not in the vehicle' and 'Disability parking permits are issued to the person with the disability not the vehicle.' The schedule is $600 for a disabled bay and $120 for displaying the permit in a timed or pay bay. Transport Tasmania states you cannot use the permit to stay in the vehicle while another person runs errands for you. City of Perth confirms the concession only applies when 'the holder must be in the vehicle, either as the driver or as a passenger.' The ACT release also flags a trap with no good-faith defence: permits reported lost and later found must be handed in, because 'anyone found displaying a lost permit will be issued with a parking fine.'

##### The permit expiry cliff, and the person who actually tracks it

- **Situation.** Ron is 84, a pensioner with significant health issues. His disability permit expired and he did not notice, because nothing in the system tells you. He parks, gets fined for failing to display a valid permit, and Maroondah City Council refuses to withdraw it, correctly noting the fine was issued properly and that he is entitled to only one review under the Infringements Act. His community lawyer escalates, explaining Ron could have a heart attack if forced to attend court. The council still refuses. - **Who feels it.** Every permit holder, but acutely those on short-cycle permits and those whose admin is done by someone else. In practice the adult child, partner or carer is the one tracking it, and they do not hold the permit or receive its correspondence.

##### The signed receipt of entitlement: I complied, and I cannot prove it

- **Situation.** You park in Melbourne, open PayStay and type your plate. Victorian plates render the letter O and the digit zero nearly identically, so you type one for the other. The payment lands against a registration that is not yours. A parking officer checks your actual plate, sees no payment, and tickets you. You request a review attaching your bank statement. The council can see the payment sitting in its own PayStay portal under the mistyped plate, a check that takes under a minute, and rejects you anyway because its decision matrix mandates 'proceed'. Your realistic options are an $83 fine or court, where a dismissal can still cost you $84 to $130. At least 1,200 Melbourne drivers were put through precisely this.
- **Who feels it.** Anyone whose compliance lives in a database rather than on their windscreen: pay-by-plate users, permit holders in zones where the permit no longer appears on the car, and residents in signposted permit areas. Disproportionately it hits permit holders, because their entitlement was never visible on the sign in the first place and now is not visible on the car either.
- **Evidence.** The Victorian Ombudsman's investigation into City of Melbourne is the strongest evidence in this territory, because it is a statutory investigator with access to the council's internal email. Scale: 345,832 infringements in 2017-18 and 286,656 in 2018-19; about 20,000 internal review requests in 2018-19 of which 4,319 were withdrawn (22.5%); and of roughly 450 reviews per week, about 30 wrong-registration errors and about 30 wrong-zone errors, roughly 13% of all reviews. At least 1,200 drivers were fined over the O/zero error (about 700 in 2017-18 and 500 in 2018-19) and fewer than 200 took it to court. Around 1,000 drivers a year elected court, about 80% after a failed internal review. A council staff member wrote in 2018 that it was 'not possible to tell the difference between an O and Zero on Victorian Number Plates.' The Ombudsman found the council's approach 'not fair', described its decision matrix as reflecting 'a mindset that the customer is usually wrong and drivers must be punished for their infractions', noted the council 'could have verified the payments', and recorded that its own Chief Legal Counsel had suggested withdrawing the first such fine. Four recommendations were accepted, including refunds. On the evidence standard, Cairns Council spells out what wins a review: documentation 'to confirm payment was made for parking at the time the parking fine was issued and that the vehicle registration was recorded correctly', showing 'the date, time, location and plate number.'

##### Plate-keyed permits: the entitlement is now invisible and you own the data-entry risk

- **Situation.** Your beach parking permit used to be a sticker you could point at. From 1 July 2026 it is a row in a council database keyed to your registration, and the old sticker stopped working on 31 August. You buy a new car in March and forget to update the portal. There is nothing on the windscreen to check, no renewal sticker to notice, and the council has explicitly made plate accuracy your responsibility. Meanwhile the enforcement side went automated: a camera car or a fixed camera reads your plate, finds no match, and issues.
- **Who feels it.** Residents in councils that have cut over to plate-linked permits, and households that change or share vehicles. Northern Beaches alone moved more than 208,000 permits. ##### The visitor permit ledger: a household's consumable entitlement and the guest who cannot read it

- **Situation.** Your parents are coming for lunch in a permit-only street. You have 30 visitor permits a year, bought at $2 each, each valid for a single day and only once you scratch off the date, each printed with a specific parking area, and each valid only where the sign reads 'Permit Holders Excepted' or 'Authorised Residents Vehicles Excepted'. ##### Carer rotation: several people, one household, a tiny fixed pool of plate-tied permits

#### Dead ends

- Digitising the permit itself, or a wallet-style permit card. Only the issuer can make a valid permit, and the display rules actively forbid a substitute: NSW requires the physical MPS card and the purple ADP permit both displayed in a specified position, Victoria requires the permit number and expiry visible from outside the vehicle, and the ACT requires the card on the dashboard or windscreen. A screenshot is not a display. The issuers are also already building their own (Victoria's state APP portal, Northern Beaches' vPermit, Brimbank's ePermit), and the brief rules out council partnerships. Hold the permit's metadata for reminders and entitlement maths, never pretend to be the permit.
- Bay availability, as in 'find me a free accessible bay nearby'. This is the most requested thing in the space and it is structurally closed to a solo developer. The working version needs in-ground sensors: Bayside's Park'nPay Accessible covers 160 bays with battery-powered sensors claiming 99% detection and about 20-second update latency. The alternative is crowd-sourced occupancy, which the brief rules out and which fails anyway because an accessible bay's state changes faster than a sparse userbase can report it. Park'nPay also has NSW government backing and distribution. Concede this one and note that Park'nPay finds bays but interprets nothing, which is why use case 1 is complementary rather than competing.
- Reporting misuse of accessible bays. Emotionally the strongest moment in the whole territory, and already served: NRMA points permit holders to the NSW Mobility Parking Scheme Hotline on 1300 884 899 and to Snap Send Solve, which is an established Australian app with council integrations ParkProof cannot match. It also turns the product into an enforcement instrument pointed at other drivers, which is a poor fit for a tool whose current promise is protecting its own user, and it invites confrontation with people whose disabilities are invisible.
- A 'proof of entitlement' card to show a stranger who challenges you. Tempting because the hurt is real. NRMA documents a member being stared at despite holding a valid permit and advises always giving the benefit of the doubt. Build it anyway and you have legitimised the premise that disabled people owe strangers a justification, and you have made the invisible-disability case worse, because whoever cannot produce the slick screen now looks guilty. Refuse this one on product ethics, not on feasibility.
- A standalone national permit rules database as the product. The research is real and valuable, but shipped as an authority it is a liability: WA proves the rules vary by council rather than by state, 79 Victorian councils administer the scheme locally, fees and concessions drift, and a confidently wrong answer costs the user $600 and possibly their permit. Even official and professional sources contradict each other, and I could not load the VicRoads FAQ directly. The rules belong inside the photo-driven flow with the existing confidence levels and a visible 'confirm with your council' escape hatch, which is the pattern ParkProof already uses for stacked signs.
- Auto-lodging permit fine appeals. Blocked three ways. Councils have no public APIs and use captchas, which ParkProof already shelved for council appeal deep-links. Appeal windows are short, Boroondara requires lodgement within 21 days. And decisively, the Infringements Act allows only one internal review, which Maroondah used to shut Ron down; an automated submission burns the single shot the user has. Drafting the letter and supplying signed evidence, which ParkProof already does, is the right depth. Pressing submit on the user's behalf is harm.
- Permit eligibility screening, or 'do I qualify for a permit'. Every jurisdiction requires clinical assessment: Victoria routes applicants through a GP or occupational therapist via the state portal, ACT long-term permits need medical certification every three years, NSW works from legislated and clinically recognisable disability definitions. An app-side guess raises false hope, carries clinical-advice risk, and adds no speed because the bottleneck is the appointment, not the form. South Australia's criteria expansion was still unconfirmed in the sources I found, which is a good illustration of why you do not want to be the one answering this.
- Treating accessible-bay time limits as a simple 'double it' rule. Worth naming explicitly because it is the intuitive assumption and it is wrong in most of the country. NSW, Adelaide, Darwin and Victoria all cap you at the posted time once you are inside the accessible bay, which produces the genuinely counterintuitive result that the ordinary bay next door often gives a permit holder more time. Any entitlement engine that defaults to doubling will confidently tell users to overstay.

##### Proving the sign wasn't there when you parked

#### Dead ends

- Airport parking. This looked like the obvious long-stay use case and it is the clearest dead end in the territory. The operator owns the entire surface: pre-booking, gated entry, plate recognition, published rate calculators, an optional Overstay Guarantee product sold at booking, online extension after you have already entered, and grace periods of one to two hours. There is no sign to read, no restriction to interpret, and no evidence to preserve. Melbourne Airport also restructured its pick-up arrangements in October 2026, moving T1-3 pick-up into the Transport Hub, which means any kerbside guidance ParkProof wrote would be stale within a year. The only residual angles are the kerbside wait zones (already free for 60 minutes and well signposted) and find-my-car in a multi-level garage, which is the single worst environment for GPS. Skip entirely.
- Interstate sign-convention differences. I expected to find a real seam here and did not. The Australian Road Rules provide a national framework and the sign vocabulary is shared across all states and territories; NSW phased out its distinctive No Standing signs back in 2008 specifically to align with the rest of the country. What actually varies between states is fine amounts, demerit point attachment and enforcement intensity, none of which change the answer to 'can I park here now'. The one genuine variation worth a line of copy rather than a feature is the No Parking two-minute drop-off allowance and the three-metre rule. Victoria's colour-coded zone system and PayStay zone numbers are a Melbourne-specific quirk rather than an interstate confusion. Not a feature.
- Tourists and overseas visitors as a product unlock. The pain is real and well documented: a Canadian tourist's photo of a Melbourne sign went viral with comparisons to hieroglyphics, and stacked multi-restriction signs are the acknowledged failure mode. But ParkProof in nine languages ALREADY IS the answer to this. Treating tourists as a new use case would produce a localisation and marketing project, not a new capability, and the repo has already correctly decided not to localise the marketing landing. This is an acquisition channel (hire car companies, airport arrivals, hostel noticeboards), not an unlock. Also worth noting that the headline statistic I found, that 34.5 per cent of West Australians have been fined for misreading a sign, comes from a parking-industry vendor with a commercial interest, so I would not put it in a case study.
- Retrospective 'where was I on this date' lookup as a new feature. This is the one I was most confident about going in, and it collapses for two reasons. First, the repo already has it: docs/ticket-validity-checker.md specs exactly this as the 'contradiction checks' half of the ticket validity checker, correctly identifying it as the part only ParkProof can do. It is an existing spec, not a new unlock. Second, the pain is receding in its biggest market. NSW councils issued 822,310 ticketless fines in 2023/24, up 49 per cent on the prior year and worth $139.5 million across 48 councils, with drivers in Newcastle and Lake Macquarie waiting around ten days for written advice, but the Fines Amendment (Parking Fines) Bill 2024 restored on-the-spot notification and photographic evidence, with narrow exceptions. The trend is toward drivers being told at the time. Build the specced validity checker; do not invent a second feature next to it.
- Weather-triggered 'move your car' alerts for hail, flooding and storms. Tempting because it uses the saved GPS location and the reframe fits perfectly ('my car, over time'). Three problems. It leans on a third-party weather feed rather than the evidence chain, so it borrows none of ParkProof's actual advantage and anyone could build it. The false-positive cost is asymmetric and nasty: telling someone to leave a hospital bedside or a dinner to move a car that then takes no damage destroys trust faster than missing an event. And I found no Australian evidence in this research of people wanting or lacking this, so it would be built on taste rather than signal. Honest verdict: a good-sounding feature with no evidence behind it.
- Crowd-sourced spot availability, parking heatmaps, or any shared database of restrictions. Ruled out by the brief's constraints and already correctly parked in the repo's own roadmap as needing cold-start solved. The cold-start problem is not a build problem, it is a structural one: the product is useful on the first park or it is not useful at all, and a database that needs critical mass inverts that. Leave it where it is.
- Council partnerships, auto-submission of appeals, and anything requiring a council to cooperate. Already shelved in the repo as externally blocked by council-side captchas and the absence of public APIs, and the brief rules it out. The Victorian Ombudsman investigation is a useful reminder of why: the City of Melbourne took nine months to withdraw a fine its own prosecutions officers twice recommended withdrawing. The institution on the other side of that counter is not a partner.
- Hospital and venue parking as a facilities problem rather than a driver problem. There is a tempting adjacent product in 'which hospital car park, which rate, which concession', and NSW Health's $51.69 million in 2024 parking revenue shows the money is real. But concession schedules, rates and eligibility are per-hospital, change without notice, and would need maintaining across hundreds of sites by one person. That is a content treadmill with no evidence chain attached. The buildable version of the hospital use case is the handover and the long-stay session, which needs no external data at all.

### Territory 5: Other people involved with a parked car, beyond the immediate household: lending, carers, elderly parents, teen drivers, housemates, and asking someone else to read a sign

Two findings reframe this territory, and both are stronger than family sharing itself. First, Australian law already assumes you keep a contemporaneous record of who had your car, and almost nobody does. Victoria's operator-onus regime (Road Safety Act Part 6AA, s 84BE, s 60) makes the registered owner the offender unless they file a known user statement naming the driver with full name, address, date of birth and licence number, before the enforcement order issues, and councils state that failing to keep a record of who was in possession is not an adequate excuse. Naming the wrong person is a criminal offence, and in Victoria the nominee can reject the nomination and bounce the fine back. NSW went further in July 2026 and abolished driver nomination for tolls entirely, so owners now pay and chase reimbursement privately, which makes a credible private record the only remedy. Second, the monitoring literature points the opposite way to what a family-sharing feature would naively build: Australian parents in a parenting-forum thread called continuous location tracking "creepy/stalkery" and tied it to coercive control, while happily accepting arrival confirmations and pickup logistics, and a moderator objected to "normalising tracking people in our lives." That is ParkProof's real wedge. It can give visibility into a car's parking session (a bounded event with a start, an expiry and an end) rather than a person's location, which is exactly what Life360 cannot offer and what people say they would accept. The strongest individual use cases I found evidence for are the handover custody log, proving a disability permit was displayed when a council says it was not (two confirmed Australian cases, one escalated to the Victorian Ombudsman), and being stuck inside a hospital with someone else while your car's restriction runs out (confirmed March 2026 case, $140, mother could not leave her 13-year-old in ED). Housemate car rostering is a genuine dead end, and so is any crowd-answers-your-sign feature, because the documented behaviour is that the crowd disagrees with itself. My web search budget ran out mid-research, so the carer, elderly-parent and condition-at-handover items are thinner than I wanted and I have marked them honestly.

#### Use cases

##### The handover ledger: who actually had the car when the ticket was issued

- **Situation.** You lend the car to your brother for a weekend move. Three weeks later a parking infringement arrives addressed to you, the registered owner. To get it off you, Victoria needs a known user statement naming the driver with full name, address, date of birth and licence number, filed before the enforcement order issues, and an incomplete statement is rejected outright. You text your brother. He thinks it was the Saturday, maybe the Sunday, he cannot remember which street. You cannot name him on a statutory declaration unless you genuinely believe he was driving, because a false nomination is a criminal offence, and in Victoria he can reject the nomination and send the fine straight back to you. So you pay it, resent him quietly, and say nothing. The same gap opens with housemates, a partner's visiting parents, and anyone who borrows the car twice a year.
- **Who feels it.** Registered owners of a household's only car who lend it out: owners lending to siblings, adult children, housemates, friends, and in-laws. Also anyone whose car is registered in their name but driven by several adults.
- **Evidence.** CONFIRMED. Victoria's Road Safety Act 1986 Part 6AA creates operator onus, s 84BE governs whether a known user statement is 'effective', and s 60 imposes a duty on the owner to give information about the driver. Council known user statement forms warn the statement 'will be rejected if you do not provide a drivers licence number or date of birth' for the nominee. A Whirlpool Forums thread quotes the official position that 'failure to keep a record of who was in possession or control of the vehicle involved in the offence is not an adequate reason unless you can prove exceptional circumstances', and posters argue a car in your possession creates a presumption you were the driver. WA's City of Stirling states owners are responsible 'unless they nominate the actual driver or person in charge within 28 days'. Secondary guidance confirms only the registered owner can nominate, that self-nomination is not accepted, that paying first finalises the matter and kills the nomination, that a Victorian nominee can reject it and the fine reverts to the owner, and that a false nomination is an offence under the Road Safety Act. Separately, NSW abolished toll driver nomination from July 2026: the owner 'will be responsible for ensuring the toll is paid, even if someone else was driving your vehicle', making reimbursement a purely private matter.
- **Source.** <https://classic.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/vic/consol_act/rsa1986125/s84be.html>
- **Why ParkProof.** ParkProof already builds the exact artefact the nomination regime assumes you kept and nobody keeps. It writes a canonical session record with GPS, a reverse-geocoded address, a timestamp and a photo of the car, then signs it with KMS ECDSA P-256 and ships a publicly verifiable public key. Add one field, who has the car, and that becomes a contemporaneous tamper-evident custody log. The signature is the whole point: the dispute is always about WHEN, a phone photo's EXIF is trivially editable, and a KMS signature over canonical metadata is not. Every other product in the family-car space produces a map, not evidence. Important design caution: a custody ledger is also a coercive-control vector, and Australia's Compass financial-abuse service already discusses fines landing on the wrong person as an abuse pattern, so the record must be mutual, visible to both parties, and never silently collected.

#### Dead ends

- Housemate car rostering and booking calendars. No Australian evidence turned up at all, the only substantive accounts are US blog posts describing informal first-come-first-served with a shared calendar, and co-oto.app already sells exactly this with booking plus cost splitting. Worse, it is structurally wrong for ParkProof: a roster needs every housemate to adopt before it works for anyone, which is the critical-mass problem the constraints rule out, and it produces a schedule rather than evidence, so none of ParkProof's actual advantages apply.
- Crowd-sourced sign answers, in any form: a community feed, an 'ask other drivers' button, or a public gallery of sign photos. The evidence does establish that people ask strangers, but it establishes just as clearly that the strangers contradict each other. On one Melbourne hydrant sign, commenters split between 'Yes, it's ok to park there' and 'The hydrant rule definitely supercedes painted car parks', and the same split recurs across every Yahoo and TikTok sign story. Building a crowd answer would replace ParkProof's one defensible answer with an argument, and it needs volume to function.
- Continuous location sharing between family members, in the Life360 shape. Australian parents in the forum thread I found call it 'creepy/stalkery', connect it to 'coercive control', and a moderator objects to 'normalising tracking people in our lives'. Chasing it would put ParkProof in direct competition with a funded incumbent, on the incumbent's terms, with a feature the target users say they dislike, and it would contradict the anonymous-by-default posture that is currently the product's credibility. The parking session, which starts, expires and ends, is the correct unit precisely because it is not a location feed.
- Automating the nomination itself, meaning filing the known user statement or statutory declaration on the user's behalf. Several states require a witnessed statutory declaration (in Queensland signed before a JP or Commissioner for Declarations), the forms differ per council, there is no online nomination at all in South Australia, and a false or misleading nomination is a criminal offence under the Road Safety Act. Build the record that makes the nomination truthful and easy to fill in. Do not touch the submission, and do not ever pre-fill a name the user has not confirmed.
- Anything built on toll nomination. NSW removed driver nomination for toll trips from July 2026, so the owner now pays regardless and reimbursement is a private matter. Queensland Linkt and Victorian EastLink still allow transfer, which makes it a shrinking, three-different-rules feature. The reimbursement framing survives and strengthens the handover ledger, but a toll-nomination feature is building on ground that is actively disappearing.
- Neighbour and strata enforcement, as in 'someone parked in my space, let me gather evidence'. Strata guidance is explicit that a photo of a car is 'often insufficient, as the owner can claim they weren't driving', that individuals must not tow or clamp, and that the escalation path runs through the owners corporation to NCAT with a cap around $1,100. It converts ParkProof from a tool that defends you into a tool for building cases against neighbours, which is a different and worse product, and it needs a body corporate to act.
- A find-my-parked-car feature aimed at a parent with dementia. The pain is real (one account has a family searching 24 hours for a car its owner could not locate) but every source I found is US caregiving forums, and the design is self-defeating: it only works if the person logs the session themselves, and the people who most need it are the least able to. It also collapses straight into the surveillance posture the Australian evidence rejects.
- Rental and hire-car fine handling. A forum account confirms hire companies pass fines on and charge the renter's card after they have flown home, but the counterparty is a business with its own established process, there is nothing for a consumer to prove, and pursuing it drags ParkProof toward the B2B boundary the constraints exclude.
- Language-barrier appeals as a product. I expected to find that councils recognise 'I could not read the sign' as a review ground, given ParkProof's nine locales, and I could not. The search surfaced only general Australian interpreter policy (TIS National, court interpreter principles, Queensland language-services guidelines) and nothing from any council or fines office on language as a ground for waiver. The nine locales remain a real advantage for reading the sign BEFORE parking. They are not, on this evidence, an advantage in disputing the fine afterwards, and claiming otherwise would be invented.

### Territory 6: The psychology of parking: what actually causes parking stress, anxiety and avoidance, which moments are most emotionally charged, what reassurance people seek, and whether "confidence" is something people will pay for

The credible parking-anxiety literature is thinner than the blogs suggest, and most of it points somewhere ParkProof cannot go: the hardest evidence is about the physical manoeuvre (27% of drivers name parking as their least confident skill, heart rate runs about 13 bpm lower when the car parks itself), which is a sensors-and-steering problem the car makers already own. The finding that does matter is Donald Shoup's "meter anxiety", the chronic dread of having to get back before the meter expires, plus INRIX's measurement of what that dread costs: UK drivers buy roughly 45 extra hours of parking a year purely as a buffer against a fine, about £209 each. That answers the commercial question. People already pay for parking confidence, badly, in wasted meter money, and governments market it too (NSW sold its 10-minute grace period as giving motorists "peace of mind"). Almost every emotionally charged moment I found sits in one place: the gap between walking away from the car and coming back to it. In that gap the driver holds no record of anything while the enforcing authority holds all of it, which is why two Melbourne OzBargain threads with 200 and 101 comments are both chalk-mark duration disputes where the poster states outright that he cannot prove what time he arrived, and a commenter notes a timestamped photo "would have resolved this dispute immediately". The City of Melbourne numbers show the commercial shape: 286,656 infringements in a year, only about 20,000 review requests (7%), and 22.5% of reviewed fines withdrawn, so the overwhelming majority never contest and over a fifth of those who do win. The competitive warning is sharp: appeal-letter generation has already been commoditised to free by Parksy (launched July 2026, 57 countries, also doing sign scanning and find-my-car) and Cato (all eight Australian jurisdictions, free), so the defensible ground is not the letter, it is the contemporaneous signed record that exists before anything goes wrong. Two academic sources I wanted (the ScienceDirect parking-assist study, a ResearchGate parking-anxiety paper) returned 403, so those rest on abstract summaries, and my web search budget ran out partway through, so a few threads I would have liked to read directly are unread.

#### Dead ends

- Helping people park the car. This is the trap the whole parking-anxiety literature sets, because it is where all the hard evidence is: 27% of drivers name parking as the skill they are least confident in, heart rate reportedly spikes over 57% during parallel parking, and a ScienceDirect field study found heart rate roughly 13 bpm lower with semi-automated parallel parking. Genuine, measurable, high-intensity anxiety, and ParkProof has zero assets against it. It is a sensors-and-steering problem that car makers have already solved and bundled. Any research into parking psychology will keep pointing at this. Walk past it.
- Appeal-letter generation as the differentiator. It is now free and commoditised. Parksy launched in July 2026 with a free AI appeal calculator across 57 countries, and bundles sign scanning, find-my-parked-car and a GPS-stamped crash report PDF, which is most of ParkProof's roadmap in one app. Cato covers all eight Australian jurisdictions free, checks facts against state legislation, and will tell you honestly not to appeal. ParkProof should keep its appeal drafting as table stakes but must stop treating it as the moat. Notably, neither competitor holds any contemporaneous record, Parksy's own calculator page says it saves your photo on its servers to 'build better parking data', and neither makes any claim about tamper-evidence. That is the gap.
- A fine guarantee, meaning 'follow our verdict and we pay the fine if we are wrong'. Commercially this is the most obvious way to sell confidence and it is the one path that is actually blocked. In Australia that promise is a contract of insurance and underwriting it needs an AFSL, or a discretionary mutual structure, neither of which a solo developer without funding can stand up. There is also an unknown loss ratio, since ParkProof's own feedback telemetry tracks retake rates precisely because the model is not always right. A safe version exists, which is 'if you followed our reading and were fined, we will write and lodge the appeal for you and refund your subscription', and that is a service promise rather than an insurance contract. Do not drift past that line.
- Predicting whether you will get a spot at the destination before you leave. This is the biggest anxiety in the territory by volume, with avoidance behaviour attached: INRIX measured 44 hours a year hunting for spaces in the UK, and JustPark's survey material reports 1 in 10 people have abandoned using the car entirely for fear of not finding a space. It is also flatly excluded by the constraints, since it needs live occupancy or crowd-sourced data with critical mass. The tempting workaround of reading destination signs from Street View imagery fails on freshness, because parking signs change and a confidently stale verdict is worse than no verdict, and it imports a licensing problem for no defensible gain.
- A towing panic flow. The emotional moment is real, the car is gone and the first thought is theft. But the information the driver needs already exists as a single phone call: Victoria's clearway contractor has a rego lookup and a hotline, towed cars go to one Collingwood impound, and the tow-away sign itself carries the contractor's details. ParkProof could add a thin and genuinely useful 30 seconds of calm by saying 'the restriction at your saved spot became a clearway at 4:30pm, so this was probably a tow, here is the number', and that belongs inside the session detail view as a few lines of logic. It is not a product, and the panic is too short-lived to build around.
- Gamifying avoided fines, streaks or a 'money saved' scoreboard. This misreads the emotion. Everything in the research points to relief and the absence of dread, not achievement, and relief does not gamify: nobody wants a notification congratulating them on sixty days without a fine, because the whole point is to stop thinking about parking. Flagging this as judgment rather than evidence, since I found no study testing it. The adjacent version that does work is passive and unsentimental, which is a quiet archive that is there when you need it.
- Proving the car was locked or the windows were up, to defeat an insurer's refusal on a theft claim. It sounded plausible as an extension of the condition-on-arrival photo and I could not find any evidence that Australian drivers actually hit this problem, nor did I have search budget left to keep looking. Marking it unverified rather than inventing support for it. Someone should check Insurance Council or AFCA determinations before anyone builds it.
- Relying on the signed record to win a mitigating-circumstances appeal, for example a medical emergency. Worth knowing because it looks like an obvious fit and is not. Victorian councils require a letter on official letterhead from the hospital or treating doctor, including the provider's ABN, explaining how the emergency prevented legal parking, and they state explicitly that a medical certificate alone is insufficient and that pre-arranged appointments running over do not qualify. A signed ParkProof session supports the timeline but cannot substitute for the document the council actually demands. Ombudsman data shows 'exceptional circumstances' was the single largest review ground at 1,954 cases, so the volume is there, but the evidence bar is set somewhere ParkProof cannot reach. Do not overclaim this in product copy.

Negligence is the most common reason these claims are refused, and you are now arguing about your own carefulness from memory, weeks after the fact, against an assessor who has every reason to find a reason.
- **Who feels it.** Melbourne above all. Owners of commonly stolen models, people who street-park overnight, households whose car is also their income. Theft from the vehicle is the same shape, since you must list what was inside.
- **Evidence.** Victoria Police reported 31,851 cars stolen in the year to March 2026, the highest figure in about 25 years and double the figure four years earlier, with about 80% recovered. Insurance Council of Australia: Victorians lodged about 12,300 motor vehicle theft or burglary claims in 2025-26 worth $240 million, more than every other capital city combined, and a car was stolen or broken into every 43 minutes in Victoria. ICA chief executive Andrew Hall: 'It's now half a billion dollars a year in car theft [claims] in Australia, and half of that is coming from Victoria.' On denials: 'If you leave your car unlocked, with the keys in the ignition or plainly visible, and it's stolen, your claim could be denied', and AFCA upheld Auto & General's rejection of a claim on the grounds that the owner left the car unlocked and unattended with keys inside. Insurers require the police report number for all claims involving theft, burglary, loss of valuables or malicious damage, plus a detailed list of stolen items with approximate values.
- **Source.** <https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-09-10/victoria-costly-car-theft-insurance-claims-revealed/107136880>
- **Why ParkProof.** ParkProof already captures the two facts that decide this claim and it captures them before the theft, which is the only time they are capturable. A signed GPS point and timestamp give police an exact last-known position and time rather than 'somewhere near the station, Thursday-ish', which materially affects the 80% recovery rate. The photograph of the car shows windows up and doors shut in a legal bay, which is direct counter-evidence to the negligence denial that is the leading reason these claims fail. Crucially the owner cannot have fabricated it after the loss, because the signature predates the theft. No tracker gives you this: an AirTag tells you where the car is now, it does not prove you were not careless.

##### 'It was already like that': proving the car's condition at every handover

- **Situation.** You hand the car to a valet, a mechanic, a car wash, a rental desk, a mate, or a buyer. It comes back with a mark, or you are charged $1,500 for damage you did not cause, and the entire dispute reduces to whether it was there before. Your phone photos exist but the other side argues the dates. Nobody signed anything at the return end, which is the end that actually matters.
- **Who feels it.** Anyone who hires a car, services a car, or lends a car. Rental is the most commercially active sub-case and the one with regulator history. The underlying moment is the same handover primitive as family car sharing, just with a stranger on the other side.
- **Evidence.** Europcar gave the ACCC a court-enforceable undertaking in 2017 after allegedly charging customers more than actual repair costs, and refunded thousands of customers. The ACCC has acted on charging for pre-existing damage, charging retail rates for bulk or unrepaired work, and undisclosed loss-of-use and administration fees. The AFIA Car Rental Code of Practice, which Hertz, Avis, Budget, Europcar, Thrifty and Sixt are said to have signed, requires a documented pre-rental inspection, evidence of damage, and an itemised repair quote or invoice. Typical disputed amounts cited: $800, $1,500, $2,400, plus a $150 administration fee that 'rarely survives scrutiny'. The decisive evidence point: 'A pick-up condition report alone does not prove damage at drop-off.' Advice given to consumers is to take 'timestamped photos or video from pick-up and drop-off, then check the phone's timestamps'. On valet and workshop handovers: the defence 'it was already like that' is 'nearly impossible to refute unless you have a pre-handover scan'. Willingness to pay is already demonstrated by a working market: Claim Done charges $49 for a letter of demand, $97 for a tribunal application, $129 for a tribunal response, $97 for a hearing bundle, against lawyer letters at $500 to $1,500 and tribunal filing fees of $50 to $120.
- **Source.** <https://claimdone.com.au/car-rental-charged-me-for-damage-i-didnt-do-australia/>
- **Why ParkProof.** The advice the whole industry gives consumers is 'take timestamped photos and check the phone's timestamps', which is near-worthless because phone timestamps and EXIF are trivially editable and everyone in the dispute knows it. ParkProof's KMS-signed record is the only version of that advice that actually holds, and it already exists, already exports to PDF, and already has a published public key and an openssl verification walkthrough. It also naturally covers both ends of the handover, which the evidence says is the gap that loses these disputes. Honest caveat: carseal.ai already ships an RFC-3161 timestamped, SHA-256 and Merkle-sealed, GPS-anchored vehicle condition record with a public verify link, explicitly arguing that 'ordinary phone photos get argued with'. So ParkProof's edge here is not the cryptography, it is that the handover is already the app's native moment and the user already has it installed for parking. Treat this as the weakest-moat item on the list and the strongest validation that the territory is real.
- **Scores.** emotional intensity high · frequency occasional · willingness to pay high · build easy

##### Who had the car when the fine was issued (nomination under oath)

- **Situation.** A fine arrives for a car that three adults in the household drive. You must nominate the driver, often by statutory declaration witnessed by a Justice of the Peace, and a false nomination is a punishable offence that can restrict your licence. You genuinely are not sure. Nobody remembers. So either you swear to something you are guessing at, or you eat the fine and the demerits.
- **Who feels it.** Any household sharing one car, which is most couples and most families with a P-plater. Also people who lend a car to friends or family, and anyone whose car was being driven by someone else that week.
- **Evidence.** Cairns Regional Council: 'If you have received a parking fine and were not the driver you can submit a Statutory Declaration to nominate the person who was driving at the time of the offence. Only the registered owner of the vehicle is allowed to nominate another driver', and the declaration must be signed before a Justice of the Peace or Commissioner for Declarations. Service NSW: 'You can be fined and have restrictions on your driver licence if you make a false nomination.' Casey Council in Victoria requires the nomination before the Penalty Reminder Notice due date and the nominee to have been 18 or over. South Australia's Holdfast Bay has moved to an unwitnessed Nomination Form, while Tea Tree Gully still requires a statutory declaration for private parking area offences. On the core problem: if you genuinely cannot identify the driver you cannot complete a nomination, and the fine usually remains with the registered operator. EVIDENCE PARTIALLY FOUND. Council and state nomination procedures are well documented. I did not find Australian data on how often households cannot identify the driver, or complaint volumes about the statutory declaration burden.
- **Source.** <https://www.service.nsw.gov.au/transaction/nominate-someone-else-for-a-fine>
- **Why ParkProof.** This is the direct, unforced extension of the family car sharing idea the developer already arrived at, and it is the one that converts sharing from a convenience feature into an evidence feature. Once a session can be handed over between household members, ParkProof holds a signed record of who held the car at any given minute. That turns a sworn guess with legal consequences into a verifiable record, and it is the only ParkProof use case where the family sharing mechanism is not just the delivery vehicle but the actual source of the evidence. Build sharing first, and this falls out of it almost for free.
- **Scores.** emotional intensity medium · frequency occasional · willingness to pay low · build moderate

##### The permit was valid and it was displayed (mobility and resident permits)

- **Situation.** You hold a mobility parking permit. You get a several-hundred-dollar fine saying the permit was not displayed, or was displayed wrongly, or had just expired and the new one was in the glovebox. It was there. It was face up. You know this. The council will withdraw the fine only if you can show the permit was valid and merely misplaced, and you have one review request to do it with.
- **Who feels it.** Mobility parking permit holders, their carers and family members who drive them, and holders of resident and accessible parking permits. A population for whom the fine is both a financial hit and a dignity hit.
- **Evidence.** NSW requires the permit be placed on the passenger side, fully visible from outside, with the 'this side up' marking facing out, and only while the vehicle is carrying the permit holder. Councils accept narrow evidence: Cairns requires 'a copy of the valid permit', Stonnington accepts 'a copy or photo of your valid Resident or Accessible Parking Permit, if it was not displayed correctly', and Yarra will only consider withdrawal 'if you were the driver and your permit recently expired or was not correctly displayed'. Cairns permits only one review request per fine and a 28-day deadline. An OzBargain thread reports a $644 NSW fine, with the appeal succeeding only after the person produced both the expired and the new permit. EVIDENCE PARTIALLY FOUND. The rules, the appeal grounds and the fine magnitude are documented. I found no Australian volume data on how many mobility permit fines are issued or overturned, and the consumer accounts are forum posts rather than reporting.
- **Source.** <https://www.nsw.gov.au/driving-boating-and-transport/driver-and-rider-licences/health-conditions-and-disability/mobility-parking-permits/conditions-of-use-and-reporting-misuse>
- **Why ParkProof.** A single signed photograph taken at the moment of parking, showing the dashboard with the permit face up on the passenger side, the bay, the sign and the time, is precisely and exactly the evidence councils say they will act on, and it is the only form of it that cannot be staged after the fine arrives. ParkProof is already taking a photograph at that moment, so this is a framing and prompt change more than a feature. The one-review-only rule makes the contemporaneous version disproportionately valuable. Worth noting this cohort has an unusually strong reason to use the app every single time, which is good for retention, and ParkProof's nine-locale coverage already overlaps with the communities least well served by appeal processes.
- **Scores.** emotional intensity high · frequency regular · willingness to pay low · build easy

##### Fines and tolls for a car you no longer own

- **Situation.** You sold the car weeks ago. Fines, tolls and infringements keep arriving because the transfer was never recorded, or was recorded late, and the registry still has you as the registered operator. To get out of them you must nominate the buyer with their details and evidence of the sale date, and a false nomination is a serious offence. All you have is a handwritten receipt and a phone number that no longer answers.
- **Who feels it.** Private used car sellers, which in Australia is a very large annual population. Also people disposing of an end-of-life car, and executors dealing with a deceased estate's vehicle.
- **Evidence.** VicRoads: 'If you don't complete and submit the notice of disposal, you may be subject to any fines, tolls linked to the car.' NSW requires the Notice of Disposal within 14 days and 'you may be liable for fines issued in the interim period'. Revenue NSW allows a person who receives a fine but sold the vehicle before the offence to nominate the new owner, and 'may ask for identifying information and supporting evidence'. False nominations are serious offences. Consumers are advised to keep the receipt, proof of payment and disposal confirmation so they can show the sale date, and to remove the plate from any toll account. EVIDENCE PARTIALLY FOUND. The liability mechanism and the evidence requirement are well documented on official state sites. I found no Australian data on how many sellers are hit with post-sale fines, so the volume is unestablished.
- **Source.** <https://www.vicroads.vic.gov.au/buy-sell-transfer/selling-car/selling-vehicle>
- **Why ParkProof.** This is the same handover primitive as the valet and mechanic case and the same mechanism as family sharing, pointed at the final handover. A signed record of the date, time, place, odometer, condition and both parties at the moment of sale is exactly the 'supporting evidence' Revenue NSW asks for, and it is signed by a key the seller does not control, which is the whole point when the alternative is a handwritten receipt. It also closes ParkProof's timeline cleanly: the car's signed history ends at a signed transfer, which is a satisfying product shape and a genuinely useful artefact for the buyer too. Lowest urgency item on the list and lowest willingness to pay, but nearly free once handover exists.

#### Dead ends

- Replacing a dashcam for hit-and-run. Tempting because the territory overlaps, but ParkProof cannot capture the impact, the offending vehicle or its plate. Dashcam parking mode does that, imperfectly, via hardwiring and a G-sensor. Claiming ParkProof solves hit-and-run identification would be caught out immediately by anyone who owns a dashcam. The honest, narrower claim is that it establishes the undamaged baseline and the time window. Say that and nothing more.
- Wheel clamping disputes. Looks like an obvious evidence problem and it is emotionally charged, but the market is shrinking, not growing. WA banned private clamping statewide from 14 December 2020 with a $5,000 maximum penalty, and clamping was outlawed in Victoria earlier still. Building for a practice that legislatures are busy abolishing is backwards. Towing, which replaced it, is the live problem.
- Claims against councils for damage to a parked car from falling branches, potholes or roadworks. The legal test is whether the council had prior knowledge of the specific hazard, which no photograph of your own car can establish. Councils routinely refuse these on exactly that ground, with one documented refusal because 'the council had not received any earlier complaints about the tree'. ParkProof could prove the car was undamaged on arrival and parked under that tree, which is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. The decisive evidence is the council's own complaint register, which ParkProof cannot reach.
- Dementia and 'where did Mum park'. Emotionally the most compelling thing in the adjacent space, and genuinely an extension of family sharing in theory. It fails on mechanics: the person who needs the record is the person who cannot reliably operate the app at the moment of parking, and a passive tracker or AirTag serves this far better. I also found no Australian evidence base at all, only US caregiver forums. Do not build it, and do not market ParkProof at this cohort.
- Family law changeover evidence. High stakes, real disputes, and contravention applications do turn on proving who turned up where and when. Reject it anyway. Courts weigh authenticity, completeness and context rather than cryptographic integrity, so the signature adds less than it looks, and the advice that actually matters is 'preserve native files and annex full threads'. More importantly, covert recording law varies by state and courts have excluded evidence and made adverse findings against the parent who gathered it. A solo developer shipping a feature that becomes ammunition in custody fights is taking on brand and liability risk with no commercial upside. The discipline of stopping applies here.
- EV charger idle fees. Fees are real and rising, up to $1 per minute at Chargefox and Jolt, and councils like Glen Eira are adopting them. But there is no dispute problem to solve: the charger's own session log is the authoritative record of when charging finished and when the car was unplugged, and it is the operator's log, not a contested fact. Nothing for a photograph to prove.
- Insurance total-loss and market-value disputes. A photographic condition history sounds like it should help, and AFCA's approach does weigh pre-loss condition. But these disputes are decided on comparable sales within a local radius and a recent window, not on proof of where the car was. The one AFCA determination I found went against the consumer on valuation grounds with no mention of condition evidence. Wrong shape for ParkProof's assets.
- Shopping centre operator liability for damage in their car park. Operators disclaim liability on entry signage, claims get pushed to your own comprehensive policy, and the evidence that would actually decide it is the operator's CCTV, which ParkProof cannot obtain and which one documented complainant waited over a month for. Your own photo does not move this.
- Rebutting a council abandoned-vehicle sticker. Real, and genuinely a 'my car over time' problem, since councils sticker and then tow vehicles that have not moved for extended periods. But a registered, legally parked car is already very hard for a council to remove, several councils state plainly that they can take no action in that case, and the affected population is tiny. The evidence is a handful of forum threads. Not worth a feature.
- Any crowd-sourced sign or availability database. Explicitly excluded by the brief, and correctly: it needs critical mass, and ParkProof's whole architectural advantage is that each user's evidence is valuable to that user alone on day one with zero network effect required. Do not undermine that.
- Pivoting the product's positioning from parking to 'vehicle evidence platform'. This is the subtle dead end. Every use case above works because the app is already open at the moment of parking for a reason the user already cares about. The parking job is the distribution mechanism for the evidence chain, not a limitation on it. Repositioning as a general evidence tool loses the trigger moment and turns a product people use weekly into one they remember only after something has already gone wrong, which is exactly the failure mode every one of these use cases is about.
