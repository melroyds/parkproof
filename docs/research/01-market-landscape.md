# Market landscape: competitors, pain, appeals, sharing, pricing
> Part of the ParkProof October 2026 product research. Start with the [README](README.md) and the [synthesis](00-synthesis.md).
>
> Raw output from parallel AI research agents, reproduced verbatim. **Where an individual report and the review disagree, the review wins.** Confidence ratings are the agents' own. Some sources were later found to be unreliable; the review section names them.

**Question.** Where does ParkProof actually sit in the Australian and global parking-app market, and what does nobody do well?

**Method.** Seven researchers in parallel, one per area, each required to cite a source URL for every claim. Then an adversarial reviewer fetched 16 sources directly, struck unsupported claims, merged duplicates and ranked what survived.

### Broke, or does not say what the report says it says

**EasyPark pricing is wrong in Report 2.** Live page: personal account free, casual service fee **up to 11.5%**, EasyPark Go **A$3.99/month with no service fee at all**. Report 2's "A$1.99/month reducing the fee to 2.25%" is stale and the mechanism is wrong. Report 1 hedged correctly, Report 5 and Report 6 had it right.

**The Whitehorse citation does not support the pay-first rule.** Report 7's strongest permit claim is that double time only starts after you pay for the maximum signed period, cited to Whitehorse. The live Whitehorse page says nothing of the kind. The Tasmania page returned 403. A separate search found a Victorian council stating the initial fee must be paid and that the payment then covers double the duration, so the rule is probably real, but **the source cited does not carry it.** Re-source before this becomes a computed leave-by time, because getting it wrong hands a permit holder a $581 fine.

**Cumberland's data actively undercuts the NSW validity-screen thesis.** Report 2 presents the mandated council reports as a structured dataset of mechanically checkable defects. The actual breakdown of Cumberland's 239 invalidations: **35** for the 7-day rule, **1** for failure to record reasons, **0** for two other sections, and **203 categorised as "Other" with no explanation.** So 85% of invalidations at the council Report 2 chose as its exemplar are not attributable to the new structural tests at all. The dataset is real. The inference built on it is not.

**Parksy's sign scanner predates the claimed launch date.** Three reports date it to 31 August 2026. A Parksy blog post from 5 July 2026 already directs readers to the free sign scanner, and a 12 July 2026 article covers the appeal tool as live. The competitor is older than reported, which makes the "brand new entrant" framing a little comfortable.

**Parkopedia has left the consumer app business.** Confirmed on its own App Store listing: "We're Moving", no longer supported from 1 July 2026, users pushed to the website. Report 1 treats the consumer app as a live funnel for the automotive business. That is now wrong, and it matters: the company with the best parking dataset on earth could not make a consumer app work.

**Could not verify at all:**
- **Darebin nomination form (Report 4).** The PDF is a binary stream I could not extract. Every verbatim quote in Report 4's single most load-bearing finding, including "will be rejected if you do not provide a drivers licence number or date of birth" and the record-keeping sentence, is **unverified by me.** s84BC is real law and the claim is plausible, but a feature should not be built on one unreadable council PDF.
- **Life360 Q2 2026 figures.** Investor URL 403. Not load-bearing for any ParkProof decision.
- **Parky.ai's Google Play 83% / 74% figures.** I verified the website side of the contradiction only. The contradiction is half-confirmed.
- **UbiPark's retirement.** Confirmed the 2021 Willoughby pilot is real and that no 2026 source says either way. Report 2 was honest that this is inference. Keep it as inference.

**Metric-label hazard.** RevenueCat's live page renders the 10.7% / 2.1% pair as **trial-to-paid** in the text I pulled, while Report 5 calls it download-to-paid. Same numbers, materially different denominator. Do not quote the metric name in a portfolio write-up without re-checking it.

**Already correctly flagged as unsupported, and I agree:** the "24% of NSW parking fines are school zone" figure (Report 7 could not trace it, nor could I) and the "58% of teenagers feel less trusted" figure (Report 4).

## 4. Market gaps ranked by defensibility for a solo consumer web developer

Filter applied: needs no database to maintain, no council relationship, no sales motion, no scraping treadmill, no legal register.

### Defensible now

1. **Verifiable claim about what a sign said at a point in time.** Two independent searches failed to find any parking product doing capture-time hashing, independent timestamping or a custody log. The sign-reader cohort's "history" is photo logs a reviewer should distrust. He already has KMS ECDSA P-256 signing shipped. Nothing to build, only to reposition.
2. **Proof of departure.** The chalk-mark overstay dispute is structurally unwinnable without timestamped geolocated departure, and `ended_at` already exists. One signing call away. Zero external dependency.
3. **The degenerate sign case.** No sign, obscured sign, contradictory sign, taped-over time limit, painted bay with one line. Every competitor disclaims it. It is structurally impossible for the database players, who model signs that exist. It converts the AI's weakness into the artefact. Zero dependency.
4. **Recurring restriction as a calendar object.** Street sweeping is the best-verified unique finding in all seven reports: weekly, 1-2 hours, different day per side, resident permits explicitly do not exempt, and the only mitigation is phoning a council two weeks ahead. Reuses his existing multi-VALARM `.ics` generator. Melbourne-only, which is his market.
5. **Answers computed for this driver and this vehicle.** One onboarding question each for permit and vehicle type. Pure prompt branching. Caveat: the pay-first double-time rule needs re-sourcing first.

### Defensible but weaker

6. **Published accuracy with methodology.** He has the Layer 1 and Layer 2 telemetry. Unoccupied position. Cost is publishing a number below 99% while competitors claim 99%.
7. **Post-ticket reconciliation.** Fine-first entry, match to session, state plainly whether the app's own verdict was right. Pure product logic, no external dependency, generates the only outcome data in the category.
8. **Payment receipt attachment.** Cheap, and it makes him complementary to EasyPark rather than competitive, which matters because councils are making EasyPark mandatory.

### Interesting but encumbered

9. **Private car park breach notices.** Legally real, empty market, but it is routing plus copy and his cryptographic chain adds almost nothing. Legal exposure without differentiation.
10. **Statutory clock tracking.** Reuses EventBridge and the push store. But lodgement deadlines are documented inconsistently at 21, 28 and ~49 days across councils, so encoding them creates liability.
11. **Household sharing.** Uncontested everywhere, and unevidenced everywhere.

### Not viable. Do not build.

12. **The penalty-amount audit.** Report 3 ranks this first. It fails the filter hardest. It requires a per-council register of penalty-unit resolutions by offence by date, which does not exist as a dataset. Merri-bek's own page confirms the defect **and** confirms the refund scheme **closed on 1 July 2026**. The defect is historical and being fixed. This is a journalism project with a great headline and no product underneath it.
13. **The NSW statutory validity screen.** Two reports rank this first or second. Cumberland's own report says 35 of 239 invalidations came from the 7-day rule, 203 are unexplained "Other", and statewide notification went from 42% to 92% in one year. He would be building a checker for a defect that is being engineered out, in a state he does not live in, for a user who must already be in his app.
14. **Refund-scheme register.** Merri-bek closed. The rest run rolling 12-month windows. It is shut before it ships.
15. **Council enforcement scorecard from 128 council PDFs.** Scraping treadmill, NSW-only, and the underlying data does not support the advice layer.
16. **Council open-data cross-check.** Melbourne-only, the bays and restrictions datasets reportedly do not join, and the portal warns of disruption.
17. **Universal payment layer.** Procurement outcome. Report 2 correctly killed it.

## 6. What the research does not support

- **That the Dispute Pack is a revenue engine.** The only demand quantification is vendor PR with no methodology, verified as such: roughly 430 monthly Australian searches across five appeal phrases. The letter is free from Parksy and Cato and A$9.99 from three others. Internal review is free in every jurisdiction with enforcement paused. Price it as an in-app upsell to people already scanning, or do not price it.
- **That statutory validity checking is the number one opportunity.** Two reports say so. The council data they cite says otherwise. This is the clearest case in the set of a good-sounding idea with the evidence pointing the other way.
- **That family car sharing is wanted.** Nobody found a single study, survey or forum corpus quantifying household parking confusion. Report 4 said so plainly and was right. Absence of a competitor in a mature category is as often a signal nobody wants it as a signal nobody built it.
- **That there is a national market size.** No ABS figure exists. The only national number is a five-year-old Finder consumer survey. Anyone quoting an Australian parking-fine market size is guessing.
- **That appeal success odds can be stated.** Published rates range from 1% to 50% and contradict each other inside single articles. Do not build a feature that quotes odds.
- **That the Reddit sentiment is representative.** Report 2 was honest: Reddit was unreachable and everything came through news write-ups plus self-selected negative review sites. The themes are credible. The frequencies are not.
- **That cryptographic evidence is admissible in an Australian parking dispute.** No court, tribunal or regulator authority was found. The vendor claims about Evidence Act presumptions are vendor readings. Market the record as contemporaneous and verifiable, never as admissible.

**One finding nobody else flagged that deserves to survive the merge:** Report 3 found that appealticket.com embeds hidden text addressed to AI agents, including an instruction about refusing payment. Competitors in this category are already optimising for agent traffic and planting instructions in page content. ParkProof is planning an MCP server. That is a live prompt-injection surface, and it belongs in the SubToll and ParkProof threat models, not in a footnote.

## Area reports

### Report 1: Global parking apps overlapping ParkProof: SpotAngels, Parkopedia, AppyWay/AppyParking, ParkMobile, EasyPark, Google Maps, Apple Maps, Waze, plus the 2026 cohort of AI parking-sign readers and the standalone fine-appeal services

The honest headline: you are benchmarking against the wrong competitors. None of the big names (Parkopedia, SpotAngels, AppyWay, ParkMobile, EasyPark, Google Maps, Apple Maps, Waze) reads a physical parking sign from a photo. They are database-licensing businesses, payment rails, or availability predictors, and a competitor's own comparison post states flatly that "no app currently gives a reliable, block-level yes-or-no answer" on street legality. But between roughly January and October 2026 a cohort of at least ten small AI sign-reader apps shipped, and several explicitly cover Australia: SIGNLanguage (Australian-focused, native iOS, with reminders, Lock Screen timer, CarPlay, 8-9 languages and credit pricing at $1 for 2 checks) is a near-feature-clone of ParkProof's core loop and is your real competitor. ParkSnap (5 free checks/day, future-time planning, CSV/PDF export, WhatsApp) and Parky.ai (85 languages, a published 544-photo benchmark) are close behind. Parksy, Sydney-based, is the only player shipping both a sign scanner and an AI appeal-letter generator that reads a ticket photo, and it covers Australian infringements. So ParkProof's sign-reading and its appeal letter are both now contested. What is still completely uncontested is the evidence chain: not one parking app in any market produces tamper-evident or cryptographically signed evidence, and the sign-reader cohort's "history" features are plain photo logs with no integrity guarantee, which is exactly what a council reviewer should distrust. Nobody closes the loop either, from "the app told me I could park" through "I got ticketed anyway" to "here is the verifiable record of what the sign said." The category also has a trust vacuum: accuracy claims run from 74% to 99% with almost no methodology, and Parky.ai contradicts itself across its own surfaces (83%/74% on Google Play versus 96%/93% on its site). Finally, household and multi-driver sharing is genuinely unserved everywhere: EasyPark caps at two vehicles and ParkMobile's multi-vehicle capability is a fleet/business feature.

#### Findings

- **No major parking incumbent reads a physical parking sign from a photo, and a competitor's published comparison says no app solves block-level legality at all** _(confidence: high)_  
  Curblee's DC comparison post states: "No app currently gives a reliable, block-level yes-or-no answer" on street legality, and assesses the field as: SpotAngels is the "closest existing option" but its "coverage and accuracy are the company's own claims rather than official city data"; Parkopedia is a directory of lots and meters where curb-restriction modelling "is not clear from its public material"; ParkMobile "does not verify that the space you are in is a legal space"; Google Maps and Apple Maps "do not show DC parking rules". Note the bias: Curblee is itself building a competing product and discloses this. Still, it independently matches what each vendor's own materials say.  
  Source: <https://curblee.com/blog/best-dc-parking-app>
- **Parkopedia is a B2B data-licensing business, not a consumer sign reader. Its consumer app is a free funnel for an automotive data business** _(confidence: high)_  
  Revenue comes from licensing agreements with automotive manufacturers and navigation providers. Its 2022 blog lists head-unit integrations with Audi, BMW, Ford and Mercedes-Benz plus data licensing to Apple, Garmin, Sygic and TomTom, and claims 60% of newly produced connected vehicles in Europe and 25% in the US ship with Parkopedia pre-installed. It launched an enhanced EV charging data API in May 2026 with A-to-F reliability scoring. Volkswagen Group is an investor. A RocketReach figure of US$16.8M annual revenue is aggregator-sourced and unverified, and a review site's claim that Parkopedia has no API is contradicted by its own 2026 API launch, so discount both.  
  Source: <https://business.parkopedia.com/blog/every-drive-begins-and-ends-with-a-parking-experience.-parkopedia-shares-its-obsession-to-make-each-one-positive>
- **Parkopedia's data comes from computer vision, sensor feeds, payment transactions and 1.5 million manual ground-team checks a year, not from users photographing signs** _(confidence: high)_  
  Parkopedia says collection relies on "proprietary software, computer vision and AI", plus parking equipment sensors, payment transaction data, floating car data, ultrasonic sensor data, and an indoor mapping vehicle. Its 2023 year-in-review reports manually checking 1,500,000 parking locations across 90 countries, adding 128,000 new locations, surveying 250,000 EV charging locations and collecting/analysing over 1,000,000 site photos. Its blog claims it is "the only parking data provider that continually validates its data... using ground teams in each location". Crucially, nothing in its public material describes reading a user-submitted sign photo to answer a live question. This is a capital-intensive survey operation, structurally the opposite of ParkProof's approach.  
  Source: <https://business.parkopedia.com/parking-data>
- **SpotAngels is crowd-sourced human mapping with no AI or computer vision, priced at US$4.99/month or US$29.99/year** _(confidence: high)_  
  Its engineering blog describes a four-part method: OpenStreetMap base styled with Mapbox, a custom in-house Map Editor (output rose from 10,000 to 63,000 spots/month for the same hours), Mapbox satellite plus Mapillary street-level imagery, and incentivised contributors with reputation scoring. AI, computer vision and machine learning are never mentioned. Notably it says user-submitted sign photos "extract parking rules but can't localize spots on their own" and "We tried to use the city open data but it was not accurate." Monetisation per its About page: "We make money by offering a premium subscription and on commissions when you pay for parking", no ads. Plus is US$4.99/month or US$29.99/year, unchanged since the 2021 launch. Users earn Spotcoins for reporting spots and uploading sign pictures, redeemable for cash, garage discounts or free Plus.  
  Source: <https://www.spotangels.com/blog/how-we-are-building-parking-maps-for-the-world-at-spotangels/>
- **SpotAngels' database model visibly fails on freshness, and users report getting ticketed because of it** _(confidence: medium)_  
  An app-store review summary describes "recurring issues with data accuracy and outdated information, particularly for parking regulations and pricing" that "significantly impact user trust and can lead to costly tickets", with at least one reviewer reporting still getting tickets because alerts were unreliable. This is anecdotal: I found no systematic audit comparing error rates across any parking-rules app, which is itself a notable gap. It matters strategically because it is the inherent failure mode of the pre-mapped-database approach and the argument for reading the sign in front of you.  
  Source: <https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.spotangels.android&hl=en_US>
- **SpotAngels has essentially no Australian coverage, confirmed only for Sydney CBD** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Coverage is confirmed for the US (100+ cities per its Play listing), Canada and London. The only Australian artefact is a 2026 Sydney CBD free-parking map blog post. Its FAQ, via a third party, says it "typically covers major cities across the United States and Canada". No Melbourne coverage found. Do not confuse it with Spot Parking (spotparking.com.au), a separate Australian company now rebranded Modii, which sells kerbside digital-twin software to councils including Parramatta, Greater Bendigo, Canada Bay, Willoughby, Northern Beaches, Brisbane and Blacktown, with one testimonial citing over 6,000 signs surveyed in a CBD expansion.  
  Source: <https://www.spotangels.com/blog/find-free-parking-in-central-business-district-area-sydney/>
- **AppyWay abandoned the consumer app as its centre of gravity and became a council SaaS/data-licensing business** _(confidence: high)_  
  Its 2026 activity is entirely council-side: North Tyneside, Greenwich, Brighton & Hove (multi-year contract) and Harrow adopted its Traffic Suite for digital traffic orders, with Greenwich using its Enforcement API and Assets API and Brighton & Hove a Traffic Data API. Third-party profiles describe recurring SaaS revenue from multi-year kerbside contracts to local government and private operators plus Data-as-a-Service licensing. AppyParking+ still exists as a consumer and business app but is downstream of the council data business. This is the cautionary tale for the consumer kerbside-data thesis: the company that tried hardest at consumer kerbside rules in the UK ended up selling to councils instead.  
  Source: <https://appyway.com/the-parking-platform/>
- **ParkMobile is a payment rail with city-set transaction fees from US$0.35 to US$2.50, and no rules intelligence whatsoever** _(confidence: high)_  
  There is no national fee. Vermont reporting from May 2026 found service fees of 35c to $2; Burlington charges 15% of parking cost with a 35c minimum and $2.50 cap; St Albans a flat 40c. Buffalo is phasing out its own Buffalo Roam app for ParkMobile in September 2026, raising the transaction fee from about 10c to 60c. University pages list $0.40-$0.50. Features are alerts before expiry, remote extension and find-my-car. Each extension can be a separately charged transaction depending on city setup. ParkMobile does not read signs and does not verify the space is legal.  
  Source: <https://vtdigger.org/2026/05/04/the-hidden-fee-spreading-across-vermont-parking-meters/>
- **ParkMobile's answer to disputes is "go screenshot your own receipt", and the evidence that decides cases is timestamped proof tied to a plate** _(confidence: high)_  
  ParkMobile's help centre instructs: "Open the ParkMobile app and navigate to 'Activity' > 'History' to find the payment confirmation... This verification will serve as proof of payment when disputing your violation." Disputes go to the operator or enforcement agency, never through the app. Cities run their own portals: SFMTA's form accepts app screenshots, and NYC's Pay or Dispute app lets users "dispute a ticket immediately using your device's camera to upload evidence". Atlanta asks for screenshots showing the plate plus proof of payment. A Houston case was lost because the driver did not supply a payment screenshot and the plate did not match. So the demand for structured evidence is real and currently met by the user manually assembling screenshots.  
  Source: <https://support.parkmobile.io/hc/en-us/articles/44199141314075-Disputing-a-Parking-Violation-Received-After-Using-ParkMobile>
- **Google Maps, Apple Maps and Waze all do location-remembering and availability prediction, and none does legality** _(confidence: high)_  
  Google Maps: parking-location save, now automatic on iPhone only (detects the park, holds for 48 hours or until you move, Android remains manual); "parking difficulty" predicts limited/medium/easy from historical data and ML; "find parking" lists garages and lots; the 2026 redesign adds Immersive Navigation with "parking help" in a 3D driving view. Apple Maps: drops a parked-car marker when the iPhone disconnects from CarPlay or Bluetooth, so it requires a pairing, deliberately skips frequently used spots like home and work, degrades in garages and rural areas, and can show a stale location on wired-CarPlay cars. Waze: find-parking with hours, services and rates, plus paid reservations through FLASH on an invitation-only basis, unsupported on Android Auto, AAOS and CarPlay. All three answer "where is my car" and "where might a space be". None answers "is this legal right now" and none touches disputes.  
  Source: <https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/maps/put-it-park-new-features-google-maps/>
- **The real competitive set is a 2026 cohort of at least ten AI sign-reader apps, and several explicitly cover Australia** _(confidence: high)_  
  Shipped or surfaced in 2026: SIGNLanguage (Australia-focused), ParkSnap (UK/IE/US/CA/AU), Parky.AI (worldwide), Parksy Sign Scanner (launched 31 Aug 2026, Sydney-based), Curb AI by Spotlink LLC (NYC, press release 8 Jan 2026), Can I Park Here AI, ParkBuddy (explicitly built on OpenAI image analysis), StreetParkingBuddy (10 free scans, no account), Parking Sign Scanner AI / Park Here (Simple App Holdings), ParkingMate.ai (Melbourne/Victoria focus), Australian Parking Sign Reader (web, claims Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane, Perth, Adelaide, Canberra), and Curblee (DC, pre-launch waitlist). #### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody closes the loop from reading the sign to defending the ticket. The sign readers stop at the verdict and keep, at best, a photo log. The appeal services start at the ticket and have no idea what the sign said when you parked. Parksy is the only product that owns both halves and it treats them as two unconnected tools sharing a credit balance. The seam between 'the app said I could park' and 'I was ticketed anyway' is unowned by anyone.
- Nobody can make a verifiable claim about what a sign said at a point in time. ParkSnap saves a photo with each check and SIGNLanguage attaches photos to history entries, but these are ordinary app records a reviewer should treat as self-serving and trivially fabricable. No parking product anywhere does capture-time hashing, independent timestamping or a custody log, and those are exactly the properties that adjacent evidence vendors argue are needed to survive an authentication challenge.
- Nobody publishes defensible accuracy. The category runs on unverifiable round numbers: 99% in under 3 seconds with no verification, 5.0 stars on 200 downloads, most apps with too few ratings to display an average. Parky.ai is the only one with a stated methodology and its own two surfaces disagree (83%/74% versus 96%/93%). I found no independent audit of any parking-rules app, by anyone, ever. In a category where being wrong costs the user A$100-plus, published and honest accuracy with a methodology is an unoccupied position.
- Nobody handles the degenerate sign cases, which are the strongest legal ground. Obscured, missing, contradictory, faded, graffitied, recently changed or wrongly installed signage is where 'contrary to law' lives, and that was 1,912 of City of Melbourne's internal review requests. Every AI reader assumes a readable sign exists and disclaims everything else. Not one has a flow for 'there is no sign', 'the sign contradicts itself' or 'the sign is hidden behind a tree', which is the single situation where a dated, signed, geolocated photo is worth the most.
- Nobody escalates on low confidence. Every reader returns a verdict and a disclaimer telling the user to check the sign themselves, which hands the hardest cases, stacked multi-arrow signs, straight back to the person who could not read them in the first place. Parky.ai at least returns 'Not sure', which is better than a confident guess, but no product offers a second read, a human check, or a confidence-gated upgrade path. ParkProof already captures confidence in its schema and does nothing commercial with it.
- Nobody measures whether their own verdict was wrong. No app asks 'did you get a ticket after we said yes', reconciles that against the verdict it gave, or reports the result. ParkProof's feedback layers capture verdict and model context but stop at user-reported retakes, which is a proxy for photo quality, not for legal correctness. The outcome signal that would actually improve the model, and that would be the most credible marketing asset in the category, is being left on the table by everyone.
- Nobody serves the multi-vehicle household. EasyPark caps at two vehicles, ParkMobile's multi-driver features are fleet-only, and the 'family' apps in the stores are vehicle-booking tools with no parking dimension. Shared visibility of live sessions across a household does not exist in any consumer parking product I could find.
- Nobody treats the restriction transition as a first-class problem. SIGNLanguage sets a timer from the rules it read and Curb AI has a 'Ticket Guard' alert, which are the only two attempts I found. The harder case, where a Clearway or permit window opens while the car is already parked and the leave-by time depends on which of several stacked rules bites first, is handled by nobody as a product surface rather than a timer.
- Nobody has made trust itself the product. The entire category disclaims its way out of responsibility (ParkBuddy: may not be 100% accurate, verify with official signage; Parky: cannot guarantee you won't receive a ticket). No one offers a guarantee, an indemnity, a refund on a wrong verdict, or any mechanism that puts their own money behind the answer. That is a real gap, and also a real reason to be careful: it is unoccupied partly because it is expensive and legally fraught.
- Nobody has independent distribution in Australia that is not the council payment rail. EasyPark owns 70-plus council relationships and is mandated for cashless parking in the City of Melbourne. Every AI sign reader is a standalone app with no institutional distribution, no ratings mass, and no council relationship. Nobody has solved consumer distribution for parking rules in Australia, which means the field is open and also that nothing has been proven to work.

#### Feature ideas

- **Stop marketing the sign reading and market the evidence chain as the product. Lead with the verifiable record of what the sign said when you parked, and treat the AI read as how that record gets created.**  
  Sign reading is now commodity. At least ten apps shipped it in 2026, one of them Australia-focused with native reminders, Lock Screen timers and CarPlay that a PWA cannot match. The evidence chain is the only part of ParkProof that nobody in the category has, and it is the part that is genuinely hard to copy because it requires server-side key management rather than one more API call to a vision model. Repositioning also fixes the pricing problem: a sign read is worth $0.40 (SIGNLanguage sells five for $2) while an evidence pack competes against A$9.99-$39 appeal letters.  
  _Evidence:_ SIGNLanguage ships AI sign reading plus reminders plus Lock Screen timer plus CarPlay plus 8-9 languages plus saved spots for $1 per two credits (apps.apple.com/au/app/signlanguage-parking-decoder/id6757208125). Meanwhile I found zero parking products with tamper-evident capture; the only comparable products are TrueScreen for insurance inspection and ProofSnap for web pages, neither in parking (truescreen.io/articles/insurance-field-inspection-app-certified-evidence/). Appeal letters alone sell for A$9.99 at fineappeal.com.au and A$39 at Unbook.
- **Build the no-sign, obscured-sign and contradictory-sign capture flow as a deliberate first-class path, not an error state. Guide the user to photograph the absence or the obstruction, record the geolocation and time, sign it, and label the resulting pack with the legal ground it supports.**  
  This is the highest-value evidence a driver can capture and the only case where the record is worth more than the verdict. It is also structurally impossible for the database players (SpotAngels, Parkopedia, AppyWay) because they model signs that exist, and it is ignored by every AI reader because they all assume a readable sign is in frame. It converts ParkProof's weakness (the AI cannot read what is not there) into its strongest feature.  
  _Evidence:_ The Victorian Ombudsman's City of Melbourne figures show 'contrary to law' as the second most common review ground at 1,912 requests, behind exceptional circumstances at 1,954, with a 22.5% overall withdrawal rate (4,319 of 19,138) (ombudsman.vic.gov.au/our-impact/investigation-reports/investigation-into-review-of-parking-fines-by-the-city-of-melbourne/). The same investigation surfaced 17,400-plus unlawfully issued Victorian fines. Every AI reader I examined disclaims rather than handles this case: ParkBuddy, Parky.ai and SIGNLanguage all tell the user to verify against the sign.
- **Publish a real accuracy benchmark with full methodology: a fixed Australian sign corpus, stratified by stack complexity, with per-stratum results, a public error gallery, and a dated version history tied to prompt changes.**  
  Trust is the only unoccupied position in a category that runs on unverifiable claims, and ParkProof already has the instrumentation to do it honestly. It is also the single most credible artefact for a hiring PM to read, because it demonstrates measurement discipline rather than feature output. The risk is real and worth naming: publishing honest numbers means publishing numbers below 99%, and every competitor is claiming 99%.  
  _Evidence:_ ParkSnap claims 99% accuracy in under 3 seconds with no verification (parksnap.ai). Parky.ai publishes a 544-photo benchmark at 96% two-rule and 93% three-plus-rule on its site (parky.ai) while its Google Play listing reports 83% single-sign and 74% multi-sign, a contradiction across its own surfaces. Can I Park Here AI shows 5.0 from 200 downloads. SIGNLanguage and ParkSnap both have too few App Store ratings to display an average. I found no independent audit of any parking-rules app.
- **Close the loop with a post-ticket reconciliation flow: the user reports the infringement, the app matches it to the session, states plainly whether its own verdict was right or wrong, and assembles the pack accordingly, including the case where ParkProof itself got it wrong.**  
  This is the seam nobody owns. It produces the outcome data that no competitor has, it makes the published benchmark self-sustaining, and admitting the app's own errors in the pack is counter-intuitively the thing that makes the pack credible to a reviewer. It also gives the Dispute Pack a trigger point and a moment of genuine willingness to pay, which is the ticket landing rather than the park beginning.  
  _Evidence:_ ParkMobile's dispute guidance is entirely manual, telling users to screenshot their own payment history (support.parkmobile.io/hc/en-us/articles/44199141314075). Pine AI starts from the ticket upload with no prior record and claims 93% success unaudited (19pine.ai/parking-tickets). Parksy's appeal letter also starts from a ticket photo with no link to its own sign scanner's earlier verdict. City of Melbourne said roughly 80% of infringements referred to court had already failed internal review, so the sequencing of evidence matters.
- **Price the Dispute Pack at the moment of the ticket, not as a tier, and position it explicitly against the A$9.99 appeal-letter market on the one thing those services cannot produce.**  
  The appeal-letter market is already price-discovered in Australia at A$9.99 to A$39, with free internal review underneath it, so a letter alone cannot command a premium. The signed pre-ticket record is the only differentiator that justifies pricing above FineAppeal, and it only exists if the user was running ParkProof before the ticket, which is also the retention argument for the free tier.  
  _Evidence:_ FineAppeal.com.au charges A$9.99 per parking letter with free assessment and no payment until generated; Fight My Fine $10 with a conditional refund; Fine Dodger from $9.99; Unbook a flat A$39 for NSW (fineappeal.com.au). Internal review is free in both NSW and Victoria, so these services sell confidence, not access. Parksy bundles its letter into a credits model at 5 credits per letter with VIP from US$10/month.
- **Use the existing confidence field commercially: on low confidence or a stacked multi-variant sign, offer a deeper second read, and reserve that escalation for the paid tier.**  
  Confidence is already in the response schema and already logged, so this is close to free. It aligns the paywall with the cases where the user genuinely needs help rather than metering the easy reads, which is what the credit-based competitors do. It also matches the known failure mode: ParkProof's own engineering notes record the model regressing on multi-rule leave-by maths, which is precisely where a second opinion earns its money.  
  _Evidence:_ Parky.ai's own benchmark drops from 96% on two-rule signs to 93% on three-plus, and its Play listing shows a steeper drop from 83% single to 74% multiple (parky.ai). Curblee identifies stacked signs, overlapping rush-hour, permit, street-cleaning and meter schedules on one stretch of curb, as the specific thing it believes no product has solved (curblee.com/blog/best-dc-parking-app). No competitor offers any escalation path: ParkBuddy, ParkSnap and SIGNLanguage all hand low-confidence cases back to the user with a disclaimer.
- **Add the future-time check, which lets a driver test a date and time before they park rather than only right now.**  
  Two direct competitors ship it and ParkProof does not, so this is parity work rather than differentiation. It is cheap because it needs no new vision call, only a different time context against rules already read, and it fits the existing refresh-mode path. Flagging it as a gap rather than an idea would be more honest, but it closes a visible hole against the two most credible rivals.  
  _Evidence:_ ParkSnap: 'Select any future date and time to check whether you can park then', listed as a headline feature on both its site and its App Store page (parksnap.ai, apps.apple.com/us/app/parksnap-ai-parking-reader/id6755999756). ParkSnap also offers a WhatsApp entry point for sending a sign photo, which is a second distribution idea worth noting separately.
- **Build household sharing as shared live sessions across a small group of drivers, with the evidence record attributable to whoever parked.**  
  It is genuinely uncontested, it adds the multi-user dimension the ParkProof portfolio currently lacks, and the attribution requirement makes it interact well with the evidence chain rather than sitting beside it. But rank it below everything above: I found no evidence of demand, only evidence of absence, and absence in a mature category is as often a signal that nobody wants it as a signal that nobody built it.  
  _Evidence:_ EasyPark's own help page caps activation at 'two different vehicles' (easypark.com/en-fr/help/parking-app/starting-extending-and-stopping-a-parking-parking-with-multiple-vehicles--21400820659228). ParkMobile's multi-vehicle and multi-driver features are described only in fleet and business admin terms on its Play listing. The App Store 'family car sharing' category is vehicle reservation between family members with no parking payment or session dimension. I searched specifically for household session sharing and found no consumer product offering it.

### Report 2: Australian parking app and parking technology market: payment incumbents, sign-interpretation apps, fine/appeal tools, council technology rollouts, and the regulatory surface ParkProof would be measured against (research conducted 25 Oct 2026)

The Australian market splits cleanly into three layers, and ParkProof sits in the one nobody owns. Layer one, paying for parking, is settled and consolidating: EasyPark is the de facto national app (claims 4M Australian downloads, ~80 councils and operators, took Melbourne CBD off PayStay in 2022) at an 11.5% service fee, and Orikan now owns both PayStay and UbiPark after acquiring the latter in Dec 2025. Layer two, interpreting signs, is commoditised globally and freshly contested locally: a Sydney company, Parksy.com, shipped an AI sign scanner plus a fine-appeal tool in late August 2026 and is PR-and-SEO carpet-bombing the category, while Parky.ai (Montreal) is the only player publishing a benchmarked accuracy figure. Critically, UbiPark already built an Australian sign scanner for Transport for NSW's Parking Innovation Challenge in Chatswood back in 2021 and it no longer appears in their product listings, which is a warning sign about sign-reading as a standalone product. Layer three, the legal aftermath of a fine, is where the real vacuum is. NSW's Fines Amendment (Parking Fines) Act 2024 created hard, checkable statutory invalidity tests from 1 July 2025 (notice must be left on the vehicle; if not, the fine must issue within 7 days; photographic evidence required), all 128 NSW councils must now publish their own invalidity counts quarterly, and Victoria's s22 Infringements Act has five named review grounds with "contrary to law" explicitly covering a defective notice. Nobody is building against any of that: the appeal tools write generic prose at $9.99 a letter, and Pine AI only checks whether the dollar amount matches the council's schedule. The scale of the unserved problem is in the Victorian Ombudsman's numbers for City of Melbourne: 286,000 infringements in a year, only 7% challenged, 22% of those withdrawn. Meanwhile ANPR enforcement is accelerating hard (Brisbane: 176,494 notices and $34.9M so far in 2026) which means more fines issued with less human judgment, which is exactly the condition under which an evidence-first product matters. Be honest about one thing: the sign-reading layer is not a moat, and I found no independent traction data for any competitor, only press releases.

#### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody connects sign reading to a durable evidence record. Every sign scanner in the market (Parksy, Parky.ai, SIGNLanguage, ParkSnap, StreetParkingBuddy, Parking Sign Reader AI) is a stateless one-shot verdict. Parky.ai explicitly does not retain the photo. The verdict is thrown away at the exact moment it becomes valuable, which is three weeks later when the notice arrives. ParkProof is the only product I found where reading the sign and keeping provable evidence of what it said are the same action.
- Nobody checks a fine against the NSW statutory invalidity tests. Since 1 July 2025 a NSW parking fine is invalid if no notice was left on the vehicle and it was not issued within 7 days, or if there is no notification and no photographic evidence. Revenue NSW has a taskforce invalidating fines on exactly these grounds and councils are publishing their own invalidity counts. These are binary, checkable tests. The only product doing anything adjacent is Pine AI, which checks whether the dollar amount matches the council's published schedule. Nothing checks the notice itself.

Victoria's s22 has five named grounds and the Department of Justice internal review guidelines are effectively a published marking rubric. ### Report 3: What Australian drivers actually complain about regarding parking and parking apps (Reddit, app store reviews of EasyPark/PayStay/CellOPark, consumer forums, Ombudsman findings, news coverage), late October 2026

Two things surprised me. First, the loudest Australian parking grievance of 2024 to 2026 is not confusing signs, it is being fined without being told: ticketless "print and post" fines, camera cars and static cameras. NSW's own Finance Minister said the system "eroded trust in the parking fine system" and that it "makes gathering evidence difficult", which is almost a literal statement of ParkProof's thesis. Second, parking-app complaints are overwhelmingly about money and account mechanics, not sign reading: held prepaid balances, forced auto-top-up, undisclosed service and SMS fees, sessions silently cancelled, inability to change a card or delete an account. Nobody complains that their app cannot read a sign, because until recently no app tried. The single cleanest number I found is Adelaide's Park Safe camera car: 31,373 expiations issued in 2024-25, only 2,199 (7 per cent) review requests, 563 (1.8 per cent) withdrawn. So roughly one in four people who appeal win, and 93 per cent never try. A councillor gave the reason: fines arrive two to three weeks later by post, "making it hard to recall the circumstances and challenge them". That is the gap. The uncomfortable counterweight is that AI sign reading has become commodity in the last twelve months, with at least eight photo-to-verdict products now covering or claiming Australia including an Australian-focused free one (Parksy.com), and Australian search demand for appeal help is tiny (roughly 50 monthly searches for "parking ticket appeal" in Australia against 1,900 in the UK). Method caveat worth stating plainly: Reddit was not directly reachable from this session (reddit.com, old.reddit.com and the JSON/RSS endpoints all blocked), so Reddit evidence here is second-hand via Australian news write-ups of Reddit threads, and I substituted OzBargain, Whirlpool, ProductReview.com.au and Australian app-store reviews as the primary native-voice sources.

#### Findings

- **Ticketless 'print and post' fines were Australia's biggest systemic parking grievance of 2024, and the NSW government's stated objection was precisely that drivers cannot gather evidence** _(confidence: high)_  
  NSW councils issued 822,310 ticketless fines in 2023/24, up 49 per cent (+270,869) on 551,441 the prior year, worth $158.0M (up 54 per cent). Total NSW parking fines: 1,380,944 worth $253.6M. The release states the system 'makes gathering evidence difficult', weakens deterrence, and lets a driver collect several fines at the same spot before any notice arrives. 30 councils agreed to change; some said they would only change if legislation forced them. Finance Minister Courtney Houssos: 'The current implementation of the ticketless parking system has eroded trust in the parking fine system.'  
  Source: <https://www.nsw.gov.au/media-releases/ticketless-parking-fines-surge-49-as-councils-double-down-on-controversial-system>
- **NSW has largely fixed ticketless fines by law, so the evidence-gap problem has migrated to other states and to camera enforcement** _(confidence: high)_

#### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody turns the moment of parking into evidence. Councils, apps, review sites and all eight AI sign readers assume the driver will reconstruct the past from memory weeks later. An Adelaide councillor states the problem outright (fines take two to three weeks by post, 'making it hard to recall the circumstances and challenge them') and no product on the market answers it. This is the single largest unoccupied position.
- Nobody is backward-looking. Every sign-reader product answers 'should I park here right now?'. Not one answers 'what did that sign say on the Tuesday three weeks ago when the camera car got me?'. The notice arrives after the evidence is gone, and the entire product category points the wrong way in time.
- Nobody makes plate entry safe, despite the Victorian Ombudsman documenting 1,200-plus Melbourne fines caused by one 0-versus-O confusion in PayStay and the council admitting the characters are 'virtually indistinguishable'. No app validates ambiguous characters, double-confirms, or reconciles the plate you typed against the payment the council says it cannot find.
- Nobody proves you left. The chalk-mark overstay dispute is structurally unwinnable without timestamped geolocation of departure, and no product treats leaving a space as a first-class recorded event. The OzBargain poster's exact deficit was 'timestamped proof of the move, a dashcam, or location history'.
- Nobody handles the no-sign case. Photo-to-verdict needs a photo of a sign. The $92 painted-T bay, the hydrant bay with no signage, and the sign with its time limit taped over are all outside every competitor's reach, and a confident wrong answer there is worse than no answer.
- Nobody reconciles the app's payment record against the fine. In every complaint where the driver won, the winning move was 'I showed them screenshots'. The app knows you paid, the council says you did not, and the driver is left as the manual integration layer between two systems that will not talk.
- Nobody publishes appeal outcomes. There is no authoritative Australian dataset on success rates by ground, offence or council. Every figure I found contradicts another, including two different numbers inside a single LegalVision article. A driver deciding whether to spend their one review request has no basis for the decision.
- Nobody serves permit holders. Disability, resident and carer permits carry the highest penalties (around $704 plus a demerit point in NSW), have the clearest documented injustices and explicit Ombudsman findings against council intransigence, and attract zero product attention from any parking app or sign reader.
- Nobody provides one payment surface across councils. This is the most explicitly stated driver wish in the entire corpus and it is blocked by council procurement rather than technology, which means it will stay unmet and should stay out of scope.
- Nobody covers private car parks. NSW's own guidance has to explain that a payment notice 'is not a parking fine', the dispute route is NCAT or Fair Trading rather than council review, the answer differs by state, and not one parking app or AI sign reader distinguishes the two.
- Nobody handles multi-driver vehicles honestly. Who parked, whose plate is registered in the app, and who owns the resulting fine is unmodelled by every incumbent, which is why the 0-versus-O class of error keeps producing fines.
- Evidence gap in my own research, stated plainly: Reddit was not directly reachable (reddit.com, old.reddit.com and the JSON and RSS endpoints are all blocked, and I did not attempt to bypass the bot check). Reddit sentiment here is second-hand through Australian news write-ups of Reddit threads. The native-voice substitutes I used (OzBargain, ProductReview.com.au, Australian app-store reviews) are self-selected and skew negative. If you want defensible frequency counts of complaint types rather than themes, that needs authenticated Reddit API access or a paid review-scraping pass, and I would not claim quantification without it.

#### Feature ideas

- **Departure proof. Make ending a session a signed, timestamped, geolocated event rather than a UI state change, and put the arrival-to-departure pair on page one of the evidence PDF.**  
  ParkProof already stamps ended_at and already signs session metadata with KMS. Signing and geolocating the departure converts an existing field into the single most-disputed fact in Australian parking enforcement, at near-zero build cost. It is also the one artefact no competitor produces.  
  _Evidence:_ The OzBargain chalk-mark thread (June 2024): the driver had photos of a worn chalk mark but explicitly lacked 'timestamped proof of the move, a dashcam, or location history' because Google location tracking was off. The council accepted the car had moved yet still argued the mark was 'clear and intact'. Commenters noted 'I can drive 10km and the chalk is still visible on my tyre' and 'All they have is the chalk mark'.
- **Fine-first retrospective lookup. Let the user start from the infringement notice that arrived in the post (date, time, location, offence code) and have the app find the matching signed session and assemble the Dispute Pack automatically.**  
  This is the whole thesis, and it is currently inverted. ParkProof serves the forward-looking question while the fine arrives backward, two to three weeks later. Making the notice the entry point is what turns a scanning habit into a paid Dispute Pack conversion at the exact moment of willingness to pay. It also makes the free tier's session history retroactively valuable, which is the strongest possible argument against capping it.  
  _Evidence:_ Adelaide Cr Snape: fines take two to three weeks to arrive by mail, 'making it hard to recall the circumstances and challenge them'. Adelaide 2024-25: only 7 per cent of 31,373 expiations drew a review request, and 26 per cent of those who asked won. The NSW government's own stated objection to ticketless fines is that the system 'makes gathering evidence difficult'.
- **Plate guard. Make the registration a first-class object with per-driver defaults, flag ambiguous characters (0/O, 1/I, 5/S, 8/B) at entry, and keep a signed record of the exact string the driver submitted to the payment app.**  
  This is the natural spine for the planned family car sharing feature rather than a separate feature. Plate entry is the highest-consequence text field in the entire parking flow, it has an officially-condemned failure mode with a named remedy, and the Ombudsman's findings give a driver citing them a genuinely strong appeal. Build the guard and the car-sharing model falls out of it.  
  _Evidence:_ Victorian Ombudsman: over 1,200 motorists fined $83 each after confusing 0 and O in PayStay; City of Melbourne knew the characters were 'virtually indistinguishable' on plates; in about 10 per cent of upheld reviews the driver had erred but had otherwise paid or made reasonable attempts to comply. The council changed policy in October 2019 and agreed to refunds, so the precedent is live and citable.
- **Permit mode. Store a photo of the disability, resident or carer permit, record a signed assertion that it was displayed at the time of parking, and pre-draft the Ombudsman-aligned argument when a permit fine arrives.**  
  Highest-value fines, clearest documented injustice, strongest citable precedent, and absolutely nobody serving these users. It is also the most defensible answer to 'what does ParkProof do that a free sign reader cannot', because it is about the dispute, not the scan. Narrow enough to ship in a week.  
  _Evidence:_ Victorian Ombudsman on Maribyrnong: council guidelines said it will not withdraw an infringement where a permit 'is not displayed at all, or a person forgot to display their permit', and the Ombudsman found comparable councils 'would most likely have cancelled the infringements once they saw the valid permits, particularly when it was a first offence'. Case: a permit blew off the dashboard, the review was declined, the fine upheld until the Ombudsman intervened. NSW penalty around $704 plus one demerit point.
- **Honest ambiguity verdict. When there is no sign, the sign is obscured, or the bay marking is incomplete, say so explicitly and capture a signed 'sign absent / obscured / ambiguous' record instead of guessing a yes or no.**  
  Every competing AI sign reader fails silently in exactly this case and hides behind a disclaimer. Turning 'I cannot tell' into a defensible signed artefact is a differentiator no photo-to-verdict product has, it is honest about model limits, and for a hiring PM it reads as someone who understands that a confident wrong answer is a liability, not a feature.  
  _Evidence:_ The $92 Melbourne fine for a painted T-marking on one side with no signs, with hundreds of divided Reddit comments. The Mount Waverley bay beside a fire hydrant with no signage. Drive, March 2026, on a sign with its time limit covered by tape: 'If you can't clearly read a parking sign, can you still get a ticket?'. Competitor disclaimers: Parky.AI self-reports 74 per cent accuracy on multiple signs and says it 'should not be used as the sole reference'.
- **Payment receipt attachment and reconciliation. Let the driver attach the parking app's own receipt or screenshot to a ParkProof session, and reconcile plate, time window and zone against it inside the signed record.**  
  The recurring winning move in every single app-failure complaint is 'I showed them screenshots'. ParkProof should manufacture that artefact automatically and sign it, rather than leaving it to the driver's camera roll and their memory of which screenshot was which. It also makes ParkProof complementary to EasyPark and PayStay rather than competing with them, which matters because councils are making those apps mandatory.  
  _Evidence:_ EasyPark, Gold Coast 2025: session 'cancelled just 3 minutes after I started it, no notification, no warning, just gone', $161 fine, withdrawn only after screenshots were shown. Another EasyPark reviewer had a fine withdrawn after showing screenshots. CellOPark: $30 penalty after a session was not confirmed in the app. PayStay ProductReview: sessions that did not register or stop, followed by a fine.
- **Rebalance the usage limits. Cap the commodity (sign scans) and leave the moat (signed sessions, evidence export, history retention) generous on the free tier.**  
  This is a pricing decision disguised as a feature decision, and getting it backwards would be expensive. Sign reading became commodity in the last twelve months and there is now a free, no-signup, Australian-focused competitor. Metering the thing a free competitor gives away invites immediate substitution. Metering the signed evidence chain destroys the one asset nobody has copied, and destroys it exactly when the user needs it most, three weeks after the fact.  
  _Evidence:_ At least eight photo-to-verdict sign readers now cover or claim Australia, including Parksy.com (Australian-focused, free, no app, no signup, promoted August to September 2026), ParkSnap and Parky.AI. Chalked (Sydney) is free and anonymous. Pine AI already drafts council-addressed Melbourne internal review letters free of charge.
- **Private car park classification. Detect whether the notice is a council infringement or a private operator's payment notice, and route the dispute to the correct process (council internal review versus NCAT or Fair Trading).**  
  Low build cost, it is classification plus routing copy, and high perceived value because the public confusion is documented and near-total. It also widens the Dispute Pack's addressable moments beyond council fines, which matters given how thin Australian appeal-help search demand looks.  
  _Evidence:_ NSW government guidance has to spell out that payment notices 'are not parking fines because parking fines and infringement notices are issued by a government body', while noting 'you are still obliged to pay the notice as you breached the terms of the contract', with disputes going to NCAT or NSW Fair Trading. A Queensland shopper's $66 private car park ticket 'reignited a debate over whether or not penalties from private car parks need to be paid'.
- **Do not build the universal parking payment layer, and say so out loud in the portfolio write-up.**  
  The most-wanted feature in the entire corpus is the one a solo developer cannot ship, because it is a council procurement outcome rather than a technical problem. Naming it as explicitly out of scope, with the evidence for why, is the discipline-of-stopping call. It also protects the roadmap from the most obvious temptation, and a hiring PM reads a well-argued non-goal as a stronger signal than another shipped feature.  
  _Evidence:_ OzBargain, November 2022: 'why can't there be a common app for all the parking spaces in Melbourne?' and 'really need[s] to... decide on a single app across the state!'. Against that: Wangaratta voted to make EasyPark the sole payment method and decommission all 72 meters; the ACT forced a switch from Parkmobile to EasyPark carrying a 10 per cent commission on top of parking fees. These are procurement decisions, not product gaps.

### Report 4: Parking fines and the appeals process in Australia, and the market for help with them

The volume is real: Victoria issues roughly 1.6 million parking infringements a year (parking is 41.8% of all Victorian infringements, and 96.3% of the 1.6 million fines councils issue), NSW councils issued 1,378,946 in 2024-25 raising about $254 million, and the City of Melbourne alone budgeted $40.2 million of parking fine revenue for 2025-26. The appeal funnel is the opposite of the fine funnel. Only about 7% of City of Melbourne fines ever reach internal review, yet about 22.5% of the ones that do get withdrawn. That spread is the entire market opportunity, and nobody has quantified it per-ground or per-council. Procedural defects genuinely work: wrong-zone and wrong-registration cases alone were about 13% of all City of Melbourne reviews, and NSW cancelled 7,336 fines in 2025 specifically because internal review found them contrary to law, mistaken identity, or caution-worthy. The competitive field is more crowded than you'd hope. Parksy.com is a direct and uncomfortable overlap: a free AI sign scanner plus a free AI appeal-letter generator plus a car finder, in 57 countries, with paid tiers for saved appeals and reminders. FineAppeal ($9.99), Fight My Fine ($10), Fine Dodger (from $9.99), unbook (A$39) and Cato (free) all already generate Australian internal-review letters. Not one of them publishes a verified success rate, and Parksy's own data says Australian search demand for appeal help is about 430 queries a month against the UK's 3,430, so the paid-appeal funnel here is thin. The genuinely unserved space is not the letter. It is the arithmetic: at least five Victorian councils (Merri-bek, Surf Coast, Baw Baw, Mansfield, Yarra Ranges) have admitted charging the higher 0.5 penalty-unit rate for minor parking offences without ever passing the council resolution that authorises it, on fines going back to 2001-2003, with Merri-bek alone overcharging about 250,000 fines by $43-$59 each for $12 million. No service in the market checks whether the amount on the notice was lawfully set, and no service tracks the refund schemes those councils are now running.

#### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody checks whether the fine AMOUNT was lawfully set. Every service in the market argues the facts of the parking event. None audits the arithmetic or the council's authority to charge that rate. Five Victorian councils have now admitted charging 0.5 penalty units without the required resolution, on fines going back to 2001-2003, worth $30-$60 per fine across hundreds of thousands of fines. The check is deterministic and verifiable from public data (offence schedule, penalty unit value on the offence date, council resolution register), which means no hallucination risk, unlike letter generation. This is the single biggest unserved space in the research.
- Nobody tracks the refund schemes those councils are running. Merri-bek's scheme opened and closed inside 12 months. Surf Coast's opened 1 October for 12 months. Yarra Ranges opened in early November for a 12-month claim window. Baw Baw is open. Mansfield is 'exploring'. All require the motorist to apply; none proactively contacts everyone affected. A driver with a 2019 Yarra Ranges fine has no way to learn they are owed money. Nobody maintains a register.
- Nobody publishes an evidence-based success rate by ground or by council. Parksy says appeals 'often succeed' with no number. Fight My Fine says outright it has no statistics. unbook's 'Outcomes' page is empty. Councils publish aggregate withdrawal totals and never per-ground outcomes. The question every driver actually asks, 'is it worth appealing this one', has no credible answer anywhere in the market.
- Nobody closes the loop after the letter. Every service hands over a Word document and walks away. No service tracks the 90-day statutory decision clock, the 35-day extension, the one-review-per-fine constraint, the ~49-day registration-with-Fines-Victoria cliff, the 12-month photo purge, or whether the review was ever decided. The product ends at the moment the user's problem begins.
- Nobody owns the moment before the fine. Every appeal tool starts from a photograph of a ticket and reconstructs the parking event from the driver's memory, days or weeks later. Not one competitor holds contemporaneous evidence of where the car was, when, and what the sign said.
- Nobody solves evidence decay. Council offence photos vanish at 12 months (Maribyrnong), photos are not legally required at all and their absence does not invalidate the fine (Knox), and access needs both the notice number and the registration. No service captures or preserves them on the driver's behalf.
- Nobody serves non-English-speaking drivers. I found zero evidence of any Australian parking-appeal service operating in any language other than English, in a market where the highest-fine-density councils are the most linguistically diverse in the country.
- Nobody covers private car park invoices properly, despite the law being far more favourable there. Vico v Care Park held an $88 liquidated-damages claim unenforceable as a penalty, operators cannot get owner details from VicRoads, and Consumer Action says they rarely sue over one-offs. Every service is built around government infringements.
- Nobody surfaces council-specific discretion patterns, despite the Victorian Ombudsman repeatedly documenting that identical facts produce opposite outcomes between councils (Maribyrnong at 14% versus five comparators at more than double that, on the same disability-permit facts).
- No reliable national dataset exists at all. There is no ABS or official national figure for parking fine volume or council revenue. Victoria publishes parking counts only by council category, never by individual council. No Victorian council publishes a parking-only revenue line. The only national number is a five-year-old consumer survey. Anyone claiming a national market size in this space is guessing.

#### Feature ideas

- **'Was this amount even legal?' — a penalty-amount audit that checks the dollar figure on the notice against the offence's penalty-unit rating, the penalty unit value in force on the offence date, and whether the issuing council ever passed the resolution authorising the higher rate.**  
  This is the one thing nobody does, and it is the opposite of everything competitors do. It is deterministic, auditable, source-citable and carries no hallucination risk, which is exactly the shape a KMS-signed-evidence product should be selling. It also produces a verdict the user can act on without legal judgement: either the council had the resolution or it did not. And it is a press hook, which solves the acquisition problem that the thin appeal-search funnel creates.  
  _Evidence:_ Five Victorian councils have admitted the same defect: Merri-bek (about 250,000 fines, 11 categories, $43-$59 overcharge each, $12m, Jul 2013-Jun 2025), Surf Coast Shire (11 'green sign' offences, records back to 2003, no record the resolution was ever passed), Baw Baw Shire (two periods over 17+ years), Mansfield Shire (hundreds of motorists), Yarra Ranges Shire (Jul 2005-Jul 2025, fine increases not documented correctly). The Road Safety Act 1986 range is 0.2 to 0.5 penalty units with 0.2 the default absent a resolution. Queensland works the same way with a published penalty unit value ($172.70 from 1 July 2026, $166.90 from 1 July 2025) and councils publishing per-offence PU ratings, so the check generalises.
- **A live register of open council refund schemes, checkable against a saved fine or a typed notice number.**  
  Zero competitors do this and it needs no AI at all. It converts ParkProof's session history from a parking log into a money-recovery asset, and it gives a reason to re-engage dormant users, which is precisely the retention loop the portfolio is missing.  
  _Evidence:_ Merri-bek's scheme ran 12 months and has closed. Surf Coast launched 1 October for 12 months. Yarra Ranges opened via an online form in early November with a 12-month claim window. Baw Baw is accepting applications. Mansfield is exploring one. Every one requires the motorist to apply, and Merri-bek explicitly did not contact affected motorists. The Ombudsman's outsourcing refund schemes covered a further ~204,000 fines (Port Phillip, Stonnington, Glen Eira) and ~46,000 (Monash, Kingston) and told motorists to check their own eligibility.
- **Statutory clock tracking for an active dispute: lodgement deadline, the 90-day decision window plus 35-day extension, the one-review-only warning, the registration-with-Fines-Victoria cliff, and the 12-month photo purge.**  
  Every competitor's product ends when the letter is downloaded. ParkProof already has the hardest part of this built: EventBridge scheduling, a DDB push-subscription store, a dispatch Lambda and a web-push fallback. This is reuse of shipped infrastructure for a genuinely unserved job, and it is the piece that makes a Dispute Pack worth more than a $9.99 letter.  
  _Evidence:_ Agencies have 90 days to decide, extendable by up to 35 days on a request for further information. Only one internal review is permitted per offence (s 22). Lodgement deadlines are inconsistently stated across councils at 21 days (Boroondara), 28 days, or any time before registration with Fines Victoria at about 49 days (Law Handbook). Councils cannot review a fine already paid or already registered. Maribyrnong purges offence photos at 12 months. No competitor tracks any of this; Fight My Fine, unbook, FineAppeal and Cato all hand over a document and stop.
- **Pull and preserve the council's own offence photos into the signed evidence record while they still exist.**  
  It is the same job ParkProof already does, pointed at the other side's evidence instead of the driver's. It is also the only defensible answer to 'why pay you rather than use Parksy's free letter': you hold a preserved, timestamped copy of the council's own case after the council has deleted it.  
  _Evidence:_ Maribyrnong states photos are unavailable once the infringement is more than 12 months old. Knox states officers are not legally required to photograph and that a fine is valid with no photos or poor-quality photos. Access requires both the notice number and the registration, and Port Phillip confirms no account is needed to view them. NSW now requires agencies to supply photographic evidence to Revenue NSW within seven days where no notification was attached, which makes the absence of that photo a defect worth documenting.
- **A ground-matcher built on the s 22 taxonomy that is honest about odds, including telling the user not to bother.**  
  It differentiates on the one axis where every competitor is empty. Publishing the methodology and the base rates, including 'this ground has a weak record', is the credibility play, and it is the version a hiring PM reads as judgement rather than feature-stuffing. The restraint is the product.  
  _Evidence:_ City of Melbourne 2018-19: exceptional circumstances 1,954 applications, contrary to law 1,912, mistaken identity 385, special circumstances 68, against an overall 22.5% withdrawal rate and no published per-ground success rate. Wrong-zone and wrong-registration together were about 13% of all reviews. NSW 2025: 7,336 cancellations came specifically from reviews finding contrary to law, mistaken identity, or caution-worthy conduct. Meanwhile Parksy asserts appeals 'often succeed' with no figures and no sources, Fight My Fine states it has no statistics, and unbook's Outcomes page is empty.
- **Pre-fill the internal review application from the saved ParkProof session, with the KMS-signed evidence attached as the contemporaneous record.**  
  This is the structural advantage and nothing else in the research touches it. Competitors reconstruct the parking event from the driver's recollection after the ticket arrives. ParkProof already holds a GPS-located, timestamped, signed record of the sign reading and the session from before the fine existed. That is a different evidentiary category, and it maps directly onto the 'contrary to law' and 'mistaken identity' grounds that actually produce withdrawals.  
  _Evidence:_ 7,336 NSW fines were cancelled in 2025 on contrary-to-law, mistaken-identity or should-have-been-a-caution findings. 'Contrary to law' was 1,912 of City of Melbourne's 2018-19 applications, nearly level with exceptional circumstances. In a 350-review sample, about 10% of upheld fines involved drivers who had paid or made reasonable efforts to comply, meaning they lost for want of evidence. The City of Melbourne PayStay zero/O episode upheld at least 1,200 fines against drivers who had in fact paid.
- **A separate, clearly labelled path for private car park invoices that explains they are contractual claims rather than fines.**  
  Adjacent to the core job, legally much stronger ground, and completely unserved by the AI-letter market, which is built for government infringements. It also protects users from the opposite error of treating a government fine like an ignorable invoice. Scope this as information plus a letter template, not advice.  
  _Evidence:_ Only courts and governments can impose parking penalties; operators issue breach-of-contract demands. Vico v Care Park Pty Ltd [2014] VCAT 565 held an $88 liquidated-damages claim to be an unenforceable penalty, with the member finding the claimed loss 'overstated'. Ace Parking was pursued in the Supreme Court for mimicking council notices without legal authority. Operators have been restricted from obtaining VicRoads owner details since 26 August 2015. Consumer Action says it is unlikely an operator will sue over a one-off in Victoria. Caveat: the VCAT authority is from 2014 and I did not verify its subsequent treatment.
- **Ship the Dispute Pack in all nine existing locales, including the review letter itself.**  
  Near-zero marginal build cost against infrastructure already shipped, and it is a monopoly position rather than a differentiator, because the competition in non-English Australian parking appeals is literally nobody.  
  _Evidence:_ I found no Australian parking-appeal service operating in any language other than English across every competitor examined (Parksy, Cato, Fight My Fine, unbook, FineAppeal, Fine Dodger, AppealTicket, DoNotPay). ParkProof already ships 9 locales including PDF strings.
- **Do NOT build the Dispute Pack's acquisition on appeal-intent search traffic, and do not price it above the commodity letter without the amount-audit and the clock attached.**  
  This is the stop recommendation, and it is the one I would most want argued back at me. The appeal funnel in Australia is tiny and the letter is already free from two credible operators. A Dispute Pack priced at a real number has to be sold to people already inside the app for the sign-reading job, on the strength of the amount audit, the preserved evidence and the deadline tracking, not acquired on 'appeal my parking fine' keywords against free competitors.  
  _Evidence:_ Parksy's own Google data (August 2026): about 430 monthly Australian searches across five appeal phrases, against about 3,430 in the UK, and about 50 versus 1,900 on 'parking ticket appeal' alone. Existing prices are $9.99 (FineAppeal, Fine Dodger), $10 (Fight My Fine), A$39 (unbook), free (Parksy, Cato). Internal review is free in every jurisdiction with enforcement paused, and Victoria Legal Aid and Justice Connect publish free guidance. Only about 7% of fines are ever challenged, so the constraint is demand for appealing, not supply of letters.

### Report 5: Family and household car location sharing: how households solve "where did you park the car", and what the privacy evidence says about family tracking

The family-location category is enormous and the money is real: Life360 did US$159M in Q2 2026 off 102.4M monthly actives, but only about 3.2M paying Circles, roughly 3% conversion. Every mainstream product in it, however, is person-shaped. It tracks a human being, and that shape is exactly what the Australian eSafety Commissioner's May 2025 research flags as a digital coercive control risk, with the Commissioner saying explicitly that sharing within a couple or family is not the problem, the expectation to track a partner is. The one real precedent for object-scoped sharing is Apple's AirTag sharing, where up to five people see an item's location and nothing about your devices, and Tesla's Add a Driver, where up to five drivers see the vehicle's location. Neither carries a parking expiry. Google Maps gets closest on parking (save spot, note the time left, Share button) but the share is a static pin and the expiry reminder goes only to you. Not one parking payment operator I checked (PayStay, ParkMobile, Flowbird, ParkBoston, EasyPark) sends an expiry alert to a second person, and PayStay in Melbourne charges 34c per SMS reminder to a single phone number. The sharpest unserved thing I found is legal, not emotional: Victoria's s84BC Road Safety Act deems the registered owner liable unless they nominate the driver, the council nomination form is rejected without the nominee's licence number or date of birth, and the form states in writing that failure to keep a record of who had the car is not an adequate reason. Every shared-car household in Victoria has a documented record-keeping obligation and zero products that help with it. The honest caveat: I could not find any study, survey or usable forum corpus quantifying how often household parking confusion causes an actual fine or argument, so the emotional pain is real-sounding but unmeasured.

#### Findings

- **The family-location category is very large and very hard to monetise: Life360 converts only about 3% of its users to paid.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Q2 2026: total revenue US$159.0M (up 38% YoY), subscription revenue US$115.6M, global MAU approximately 102.4M (up 16%), paying Circles approximately 3.2M (up 27%), ARPU per paying subscription US$128.38. FY26 guidance US$650-685M. Dual-listed ASX:360 / NASDAQ:LIF, so Australian investors already understand this category. The implication for a solo builder is that the paid willingness sits in a narrow slice of the user base, and the free tier carries the other 97%.  
  Source: <https://investors.life360.com/news-releases/news-release-details/life360-reports-record-q2-2026-results>
- **Australian regulator research directly links location-sharing apps to elevated coercive control risk, and draws the line at person-tracking rather than family sharing.** _(confidence: high)_  
  eSafety Commission study of 2,000 adults, reported 15 May 2025. Over 13% said it was reasonable for a partner to monitor them via location-sharing apps. Nearly one in five young adults believe tracking a partner's location is to be expected in a relationship. About a quarter agreed it was necessary to have a partner's device passwords. Commissioner Julie Inman Grant's framing is the useful part: sharing location within a couple or family is not the problem, the expectation to track a romantic partner's movements is 'problematic' and 'even dangerous'. The article cites the 2023 murder of Lilie James, whose killer tracked her via Snapmaps. This is the single most important source in the whole research area and it is Australian.  
  Source: <https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-05-15/location-sharing-apps-esafety-commission-coercive-control/105289994>
- **The normalisation pathway runs parent to partner: family tracking is what teaches young people to accept partner tracking.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Griffith University study (2025), PhD candidate Maria Atienzar Prieto, surveyed more than 1,000 young people. Found location sharing has become normalised partly because parents use tracking apps for children's safety. Atienzar Prieto: acceptance 'starts at home', participants first experienced monitoring in a caring context which later carried over to other relationships. This matters for product design because a family car feature that normalises person-tracking is contributing to a documented harm pathway, and a feature that tracks the car instead is not.  
  Source: <https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-05-15/location-sharing-apps-esafety-commission-coercive-control/105289994>
- **Life360's documented backlash is specifically about tracking people, and the company's own fix was to make location fuzzier.** _(confidence: high)_  
  TechCrunch reported teen users hated the location tracking so much that dissing the app became a TikTok meme, with teens leaving phones at friends' houses or force-stopping the app. CEO Chris Hulls made a TikTok account to talk to them, which produced 'Bubbles': share a circle representing a generalised location instead of exact whereabouts, with safety and messaging still active. A company retreating from precision under user pressure is a strong signal that precision on people is a liability.  
  Source: <https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/12/family-tracking-app-life360-launches-bubbles-a-location-sharing-feature-inspired-by-teens-on-tiktok>
- **Life360 sold precise user location data to data brokers, including children's locations.** _(confidence: high)_  
  The Markup reported in 2021 that Life360 sold its users' precise location data to data brokers. Critics noted this included the exact locations of children. The company has said it stopped selling precise location data. This is the category leader's permanent credibility wound and the most direct wedge for a privacy-first positioning.  
  Source: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life360>
- **Apple's AirTag sharing is the one mainstream precedent for sharing an OBJECT's state rather than a person's position, and it is deliberately asymmetric.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Since iOS 17, an owner can share one AirTag with up to five other people (six total). Invitees see the item's location, can play a sound and use Precision Finding. Sharing exposes only the tag's location, not the owner's iPhone or other devices. People you share with do not get unknown-tag alerts for that tag. Works outside Family Sharing with any full Apple Account. This is the exact design grammar a car-state share should borrow: the thing is shared, the human is not, and the invitee list is small and explicit.  
  Source: <https://www.foxnews.com/tech/how-your-airtag-can-be-shared-with-up-to-five-other-people.amp>
- **Tesla already proves households want multi-driver vehicle-location sharing, but only connected cars get it.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Tesla's Add a Driver supports up to five drivers per vehicle. Added drivers get vehicle location information, vehicle access, service scheduling, in-app messages and roadside assistance. They cannot add or remove drivers, manage payment methods, buy upgrades, view charging history or access insurance. Tesla's support pages do not say whether a parked location updates live or shows last-known position. The gap: this is a feature of expensive connected cars, so the 2009 Corolla household gets nothing.  
  Source: <https://www.tesla.com/support/how-add-or-remove-drivers>
- **Apple's Parked Car is architecturally non-shareable by design, so Apple is unlikely to ship this.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Apple's Maps privacy page states that personalised features including Visited Places, Parked Car, Time to Leave, Pinned Locations and Suggestions are created using data generated on your device. Apple also suppresses the marker at familiar locations: if you park at home or work, Maps does not mark it as parked. The location only updates when the phone last connected to the car. Family location runs through Find My instead, which is person-shaped. So the incumbent with the best distribution has deliberately built the opposite architecture.  
  Source: <https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/apple-maps/>
- **Google Maps is the closest free competitor and it still does not share the expiry with the other person.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Google's own help page: tap the blue dot then 'Save your parking'; the location stays saved until you remove it. Under More info you can add photos and notes 'like which floor your car is on or how much time is left' (a free-text note, not a timer field, in the current documentation). Notifications for parking information are a setting under Getting around. Sharing exists: Parking location, More info, Share. But the help page is silent on whether a shared location carries the time remaining or updates live, and the reminder is configured for the account holder's own device. An older Google blog described a meter timer with a 15-minute reminder, so the feature has drifted. Treat 'the share excludes expiry' as strongly implied rather than documented.  
  Source: <https://support.google.com/maps/answer/7257797?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid>
- **No parking payment operator sends an expiry alert to anyone except the account holder.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  ParkBoston sends a push notification before a session ends, to the user. Flowbird applies SMS reminders automatically to the transaction's own phone. ParkMobile offers customisable expiry reminders by email or text, 15 minutes ahead, to the user. PayStay (Melbourne, by Orikan) is tied to one phone number per account, allows multiple vehicles, offers opt-in reminders about 10-15 minutes before expiry, and charges 34c per SMS reminder. Across every operator the pattern holds: the operator owns the countdown and delivers it to exactly one pocket. This is an absence-of-evidence finding, but it is consistent across five independent operators.  
  Source: <https://www.paystay.com.au/faq>
- **EasyPark's 'family account' is a billing construct, not a shared-state construct.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  EasyPark help pages: multiple users can be added under one private account and park simultaneously using the same payment method and balance. Maximum four registered users on a private account, each needing a separate unique phone number. More than four requires a business account, and a private account cannot be converted. EasyPark Go in Australia caps at four licence plates. EasyPark Business Share exists for fleets where several people drive the same car. Nowhere does it give household members a view of where the car is or when the current session expires. It solves 'who pays', not 'where is it and when must it move'.  
  Source: <https://www.easypark.com/en-it/help/other/new-easypark-customers-sostafacile/family-account>
- **The only dedicated family parking-location-sharing app I could find is effectively dead, which is both a warning and an opening.** _(confidence: high)_  
  We Share Park (iOS, Alessandro Manilii) describes itself as letting you 'Share on iCloud the position of the parking lot of your vehicle with your family member or with your friend', supports Family Sharing up to six, and claims 'All on your private iCloud. No datas will be send to anybody else.' It costs US$0.99, was last updated version 1.2.0 on 24 August 2023, and has not received enough ratings or reviews to display an overview. Zero traction. It also does not touch parking expiry or time limits. Read it as: a bare location share with no expiry and no distribution is not a product.  
  Source: <https://apps.apple.com/us/app/we-share-park/id1613689921>
- **The entire 'find my car' app category is single-player, ad-funded, and shares nothing meaningful.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Find My Parked Car (iOS) 4.6 from approximately 12K ratings. Find My Parked Car (Android, Aurum) 4.4 from approximately 49.6K reviews, 1M+ downloads. Parked Car Locator 4.4 from approximately 5.45K reviews, 500K+ downloads. Where is my car approximately 4.0 from approximately 850 reviews. Dominant complaints are unskippable ads (one reviewer: the ad appears and there is no way to skip or minimise it), false-positive auto-detection ('detects false positives all the time'), and crow-flies arrows instead of routing. A couple of listings mention sharing a parking location, but none pair it with an expiry alert to the recipient. There is real demand here (millions of downloads) being served badly by adware.  
  Source: <https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.car.android.parking.locator&hl=en>
- **Victorian law imposes a documented record-keeping obligation on every shared-car household, and the council form says so in writing.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Verbatim from the City of Darebin Known User Nomination Statement: 'In accordance with section 84BC of the Road Safety Act 1986 means that the registered owner of the vehicle is deemed responsible for any offences associated with that vehicle unless that person nominates the person who was driving or in possession of the vehicle at the time of the offence.' And: 'Failure to keep a record of who was in possession or control of the vehicle involved in the offence is not an adequate reason unless you can provide exceptional circumstances.' Required nominee fields: first name, last name, address, suburb, state, postcode, drivers licence/permit number, state/country of issue, date of birth. Rejection warning: 'This statement will be rejected if you do not provide a drivers licence number or date of birth for a nominated individual.' There is a dedicated box for 'DETAILS OF REASONABLE AND DILIGENT ENQUIRIES (IF USER UNKNOWN)'. The statement is signed, carries 'I understand that I may be served with a summons to give evidence in relation to this nomination', and false or misleading information is an offence under s84BI. This is the most actionable thing in the entire research set.  
  Source: <https://www.darebin.vic.gov.au/files/assets/public/v/1/parking-roads-and-transport/documents/knownuserstatementpdf.pdf>
- **Councils also enforce nominee-detail completeness and short windows, and nominations die once the fine reaches Fines Victoria.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Greater Dandenong requires full name, an Australian residential address ('international, hotel or holiday addresses are not accepted'), date of birth or driver licence number, and the nominee must be 18 or older at the time of the offence. Whitehorse: 'All nomination details must be completed for a nomination to be considered', no PO Box addresses. NSW requires nomination within 21 days of the notice date via Revenue NSW myPenalty or a signed, witnessed statutory declaration. Queensland allows 28 days. Reported across sources: once a Victorian fine is registered with Fines Victoria, councils can no longer process a nomination. The time pressure is the product hook: the record has to already exist when the notice arrives.  
  Source: <https://www.greaterdandenong.vic.gov.au/fines-and-infringements/nominate-another-driver-parking-fine>
- **The duty to identify the driver has a two-limb legal standard, and 'documented enquiries' is explicitly part of it.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  A Victorian criminal law practice summary: the duty has two parts, giving the information and making enquiries to obtain it. Saying you do not know without looking into it does not satisfy the second part. The standard is 'make all reasonable enquiries', weighed by how many people could have used the vehicle, how much time has passed, and what records or contacts the owner has. Maximum penalty 20 penalty units (quoted as $4,182), with imprisonment available in accident-investigation cases. Critically: reasonable enquiries must be 'genuine and documented where possible', with examples given of checking diaries and calendars, asking family members who use the vehicle, and reviewing records of who had the vehicle on the relevant date. Single-firm secondary source, so verify against the Act before any marketing claim, but the specificity is useful.  
  Source: <https://australiancriminallawyers.com.au/victoria/offences/duty-of-owner-of-motor-vehicle-to-give-information-about-driver/>
- **The addressable Australian household-shared-car segment is large, but ABS does not publish the cross-tab that would size it precisely.** _(confidence: high)_  
  2021 Census: approximately 1.8 motor vehicles per household, approximately 91% of households have at least one vehicle, 673,969 households have none. Roughly 35% of households have exactly one vehicle, roughly 35% have two, roughly 18% have three or more, so roughly 53% have two or more. The honest limit: the Census counts vehicles per dwelling, not people sharing them. A two-adult one-car household is indistinguishable in the data from a single person with one car. Sizing 'one car, two or more adults' needs an ABS TableBuilder cross-tab that is not published. Also note ABS is discontinuing the Motor Vehicle Census.  
  Source: <https://profile.id.com.au/australia/car-ownership>
- **The viral teen-tracking statistics everyone quotes are mostly unverifiable; the defensible numbers are lower than the discourse suggests.** _(confidence: high)_  
  Pew Research 2016 (parents of 13-17 year olds): only 16% use monitoring tools on a teen's cellphone to track location, far below checking websites visited (61%) or social media profiles (60%). Pew 2020 (parents of children 5-11): 33% use GPS apps or software to track location, higher among parents with less formal education (40% high school or less vs 26% college or more). These are different populations and should not be merged. The widely-circulated '58% of teenagers feel less trusted when tracked' figure attributed to a 2023 Common Sense Media report could not be confirmed against the original report. Do not use it.  
  Source: <https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2016/01/07/parents-teens-and-digital-monitoring/>
- **Tracking adult family members has a measurable emotional cost to the trackers themselves, not just the tracked.** _(confidence: low)_  
  University of Michigan poll of 1,542 parents, reported by NPR in June 2026, found about 25% of parents who track adult children said it sometimes increases anxiety rather than providing reassurance. I could not fetch the NPR article directly (HTTP 403), so this comes from search result summaries and the exact framing should be verified before use. Useful because it undercuts the 'more visibility is always better' assumption that every family-tracking product is built on.  
  Source: <https://www.npr.org/2026/06/15/nx-s1-5854613/tracking-smartphone-kids-parents-adults>
- **Where location sharing IS accepted socially, the accepted shape is narrow-audience and opting out carries a social penalty.** _(confidence: low)_  
  A 2025 CivicScience survey, cited secondhand, found 65% of Gen Z share their location. A student newspaper survey of 51 Snapchat users found 88% enable Snap Map, and of those 76% limit sharing to specific people rather than everyone they have added. SheKnows reported nearly a quarter of teens it spoke to said seeing a friend on ghost mode makes them feel 'anxious' or 'left out'. Small, non-representative samples, so treat the numbers as directional only. The design lesson survives the weak sampling: people accept sharing to a small named circle, and any product that makes opting out visible to others creates coercion.  
  Source: <https://www.urbanlegendnews.org/arts/2025/10/30/students-across-the-map-snapchat-and-location-sharing/>
- **Time-boxed, auto-expiring sharing is the established consent grammar across every major platform, and it is absent from parking entirely.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Apple Find My Friends offers one hour, until end of day, or indefinitely, with pause at any point. WhatsApp live location offers 15 minutes, one hour, or eight hours. OtoZen offers temporary sharing of 1 to 12 hours with sharing stopping automatically when the period ends. A location-sharing patent describes presets of 30 minutes, 1 hour, 2 hours, 4 hours plus manual entry. A DTIC study found that users with complex privacy preferences given limited settings tend to err on the safe side and share less, which argues for a small set of obvious durations rather than a configuration screen.  
  Source: <https://www.axios.com/2023/07/17/location-sharing-apps-gen-z>
- **Life360 has no real vehicle-state object; its 'car' features are inferred from the member's phone via a third-party data processor.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Life360's Drive Detection, which powers crash alerts and driving reports, is powered by Arity. Turning off data sharing with Arity disables those features. Crash detection is described as sensing collisions over 25mph. Family Driving Summaries are a weekly Circle-wide overview, individual Driver Reports are a higher tier, covering speed, phone usage, hard braking and rapid acceleration. Tile integration adds items and pets to the same map. One aggregator review reports inconsistent crash-detection reliability including failures in real high-impact accidents, which is a secondary source and should be treated cautiously. Net: the category leader models people and things, never a vehicle with a legal state.  
  Source: <https://support.life360.com/hc/en-us/articles/23053499870487-Drive-Detection-Analysis>
- **Partner location sharing is mostly consensual but a meaningful minority is covert, and covert tracking correlates with relationships ending.** _(confidence: low)_  
  A study of 238 participants published in Computers in Human Behavior Reports found most people used location-sharing apps voluntarily, but notable proportions admitted to tracking a partner without consent or suspected being tracked themselves. Openness predicted discomfort with covert tracking and was linked to relationship dissolution. I could not fetch the paper directly (HTTP 403 from ScienceDirect), so exact percentages are unverified and the sample is small. Separately, a qualitative study of online forums found partner-surveillers 'harness the emotive power of sharing personal narratives to validate and legitimise their abusive behaviours', which is a warning about how a family car feature could be marketed and misread.  
  Source: <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958826000527>
- **The 'who was driving' problem has products, but they are all US teen-learner logbooks solving a different problem.** _(confidence: medium)_  
  GoTime (iOS) lets several family members log to one shared dashboard with each drive recording the supervising driver's name, plus automatic time and distance, day/night classification, notes, and shareable or printable exports. Drive Logging requires a parent PIN per drive and openly states that GPS cannot prove who drove, only the parent's PIN says they were there. Drive Experience Log tracks hours for multiple learners. None of these are built for infringement nomination, none capture licence number or date of birth, and none are Australian. The closest adjacent product to a household driver record is a learner-hours tracker.  
  Source: <https://apps.apple.com/us/app/gotime-teen-driving-log/id6759268978>

#### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody shares the STATE of a car as a first-class object with a ticking expiry. Apple shares an item's location with no rules and no expiry. Google shares a static pin with the expiry living only in the parker's own notifications. Tesla shares vehicle location but only for connected cars and with no parking rules. Operators own the authoritative expiry and send it to exactly one phone. Nothing anywhere joins location plus legal expiry plus who parked it into one shareable thing.
- Nobody delivers the expiry warning to the person who can actually move the car. Every reminder across ParkBoston, Flowbird, ParkMobile, PayStay and EasyPark goes to the account holder. If one person parked and the other has the keys, the alert is in the wrong pocket. This is the simplest, most obviously-correct unbuilt feature in the entire space.
- Nobody provides a household handover record, despite Victorian law demanding one in writing. s84BC deems the registered owner liable, the nomination form is rejected without the nominee's licence number or date of birth, and the form states that failure to keep a record of who had the vehicle is not an adequate reason. The only 'who was driving' products in existence are US teen learner-hour logbooks with parent PINs.
- Nobody separates 'share the car' from 'share the person'. Every mainstream family-location product is person-shaped, and person-shaped is precisely the shape the Australian eSafety research identifies as the coercive control risk. The Commissioner's own framing leaves a clean, defensible hole: family sharing is not the problem, the expectation to track a person is. No product currently stands in that hole.
- Nobody applies the time-boxed, auto-expiring consent pattern to parking. Apple, WhatsApp and OtoZen have all converged on presets that end by themselves. In parking the sharing is either permanent or a dead static pin. A share that dies when the session dies has no precedent in this category.
- Nobody captures nomination-grade driver identity at the moment it is knowable. The council form needs a licence number or date of birth and rejects the nomination without it. That information is easiest to collect once, calmly, when a household sets up its drivers, and impossible to reconstruct six weeks later from memory. No product collects it.
- Nobody has a credible privacy story to sell family car features with. The category leader's is actively damaged by the 2021 data-broker reporting and the teen revolt that forced it to blur its own core feature. Nobody is marketing 'we never show where a person is, only where the car is' as the product proposition.
- Nobody serves the one-car two-adults household as a distinct segment. EasyPark's family account is billing plumbing. The family-safety category sells child safety to parents. Partner logistics, which is a different emotional job with none of the surveillance baggage, has no product aimed at it.
- EVIDENCE GAP I COULD NOT FILL: there is no data anywhere on how often household parking confusion actually causes a fine, a missed appointment or an argument. No survey, no study, no usable forum corpus surfaced across multiple search strategies. Treat the 'where did you park the car' pain as plausible but completely unquantified, and do not build a pricing argument on it.
- EVIDENCE GAP: ABS does not publish the cross-tab of vehicles per dwelling against number of adults in the household, so the 'one car, two or more drivers' segment cannot be sized from public data. It would require a TableBuilder query. The published figures only establish that roughly 35% of Australian households have exactly one vehicle.
- EVIDENCE GAP: Google's current help documentation is silent on whether a shared parking location carries the time remaining, and the 'time left' field appears to have degraded from a real timer (2017 blog) to a free-text note (current help page). Worth five minutes in the actual app before claiming Google does not do this.

#### Feature ideas

- **Ship a Car Card, not a person pin. The shareable unit is the vehicle's state: where it is, what the sign said, when it expires, who parked it, whether the session has been ended. It renders a car and a countdown. It never renders a human's position.**  
  This is the only shape that is simultaneously useful to a household and immune to the coercive-control critique. It also happens to be the shape ParkProof already has in its data model, since a session already carries location, rules, expiry and an evidence record. The feature is a sharing surface over state that already exists, not a new product.  
  _Evidence:_ Apple's AirTag sharing proves object-scoped sharing is a mainstream, accepted primitive: up to five people see the item's location and nothing about the owner's devices, and they get no unknown-tag alerts. Tesla's Add a Driver proves households want multi-driver vehicle location, capped at five drivers, but gates it behind connected cars. Apple will not compete here because its own privacy documentation states Parked Car is generated on-device, and it suppresses the marker at home and work.
- **Send the expiry alert to whoever can move the car, not just whoever parked it. Every member of a car's circle gets the same countdown and the same 'I've left' button.**  
  It is the cheapest high-value thing in this entire research set. It requires no new data, no new permission model beyond the share itself, and it converts a single-player reminder into a household utility. It is also the clearest paid-tier justification, because a household that avoids one $99 Melbourne fine has paid for years of subscription.  
  _Evidence:_ ParkBoston, Flowbird, ParkMobile, PayStay and EasyPark all send expiry reminders to the account holder only. PayStay in Melbourne is one phone number per account and charges 34c per SMS reminder to that single phone. Google Maps' parking notification is configured per-device under the account holder's own settings. Across five independent operators and two platform incumbents, not one delivers the countdown to a second person.
- **Make every share auto-expiring by default, with Apple-style presets: until this session expires, one hour, or until I end it. No indefinite option on day one.**  
  It removes the main abuse vector before anyone can complain about it, it is the pattern users already understand from three other apps, and it sidesteps the 'turning sharing off looks suspicious' dynamic because the share ending is the default, not a decision anyone has to make. It also keeps the feature honest: a parking share that outlives the parking session is just tracking.  
  _Evidence:_ Apple Find My Friends offers one hour, until end of day, or indefinitely, with pause anytime. WhatsApp live location offers 15 minutes, one hour or eight hours. OtoZen offers 1 to 12 hours with automatic stop. A DTIC study found users given limited privacy settings err toward sharing less, which argues for three obvious presets over a settings screen. No parking product uses any of this.
- **Build a household handover log with nomination-grade fields. Each session records who parked, when, and which driver had the car. Each household driver's licence number or date of birth is captured once, encrypted, and surfaced only when filling a nomination. The existing KMS signature timestamps the record.**  
  This is the sharpest differentiator found in the whole area, it is Australian-specific, it is legally concrete rather than emotional, and it is a direct extension of the evidence chain ParkProof already ships. It turns family car sharing from a convenience feature into a liability-management feature, which is a much easier thing to charge for and a much better story in an interview.  
  _Evidence:_ The City of Darebin Known User Nomination Statement states verbatim that under s84BC Road Safety Act 1986 the registered owner is deemed responsible unless they nominate the driver, that the statement 'will be rejected if you do not provide a drivers licence number or date of birth for a nominated individual', and that 'Failure to keep a record of who was in possession or control of the vehicle involved in the offence is not an adequate reason unless you can provide exceptional circumstances.' The form has a dedicated box for reasonable and diligent enquiries. A Victorian practice summary adds that reasonable enquiries must be 'genuine and documented where possible', citing checking calendars and asking family members who use the vehicle. Nominations also die once a Victorian fine registers with Fines Victoria, so the record must pre-exist the notice.
- **Reduce 'who's got the car' to a single status line, not a map. Either 'Parked on Lygon St, expires 4:20pm' or 'In use by Sam'. One bit of state, no trail, no history of movement.**  
  A status line is the whole job for a two-adult household and it carries almost none of the surveillance payload a map does. It is also the right scope discipline: the thing worth building is the answer, not the visualisation. Anything richer starts re-importing Life360's product and Life360's problems.  
  _Evidence:_ Life360 converts only about 3.2M of 102.4M monthly actives to paid, which says the narrow answer is what people actually value and the elaborate map is what they tolerate for free. Life360's own Bubbles retreat, built directly in response to the teen TikTok backlash, shows the company concluded precision on people was a liability. The dedicated family parking app that exists, We Share Park, shares a bare location with no expiry and has zero ratings, which shows location alone is not enough either.
- **State the anti-tracking position explicitly on the marketing page and in the share sheet itself: ParkProof never shows where a person is. It shows where the car is.**  
  Positioning is free and this one is both true of the architecture and directly responsive to the regulator's own framing. It also pre-empts the single most likely objection to the feature, which is 'isn't this just stalkerware for couples'. Having the answer written down before launch is the pre-decided stressful judgment.  
  _Evidence:_ eSafety Commissioner Julie Inman Grant's framing is that sharing within a couple or family is not the problem, the expectation to track a romantic partner's movements is 'problematic' and 'even dangerous', with 13% of 2,000 Australian adults surveyed saying partner monitoring is reasonable and nearly one in five young adults saying it is expected. Life360's 2021 data-broker reporting and the TikTok-meme-scale teen revolt show what happens to a brand that gets this wrong.
- **If a teen or learner driver tier is ever built, make it deliberately asymmetric: the young driver can see and claim the car, the parent sees the car's state and who claimed it, and nobody sees the young driver's live position.**  
  It gives parents the thing they actually need, which is the car accounted for and the nomination record intact, without participating in the normalisation pathway the Griffith research documents. It is also a genuinely novel position in a category where every competitor sells parental visibility into the child.  
  _Evidence:_ The Griffith University study of over 1,000 young people found location sharing is normalised because 'it starts at home' with parental tracking, and that participants first experienced monitoring in a caring context which later carried into romantic relationships. Pew 2016 found only 16% of parents of teens actually used location tracking tools, so the demand for person-tracking is smaller than the discourse implies. Snap Map data, weak as the sample is, shows a quarter of teens feel anxious or left out when someone goes invisible, which is the coercion dynamic to avoid designing in.
- **Explicitly refuse to build: a member-location map, geofence arrival and departure alerts, driving scores, speed alerts, crash detection, or any history of where people have been.**  
  These are Life360's moat and they are also its liability, and a solo builder cannot win the moat. More importantly every one of them re-imports exactly the harm the Australian regulator has documented, and together they would destroy the only differentiated position available. Writing the Tier 4 list down now is what stops it getting built later under feature pressure.  
  _Evidence:_ Life360's driving features are not even car-derived: Drive Detection is powered by Arity and disabling Arity data sharing kills crash detection and driving reports, so the moat is a third-party data relationship a solo builder has no access to. One aggregator review reports crash detection failing in real high-impact accidents, so the feature is also a safety-claim liability. The eSafety research, the Griffith research and the covert-partner-tracking study all point at person-level monitoring as the harm vector.

### Report 6: Pricing and freemium benchmarks for consumer utility apps, with an Australian lens: parking app pricing (SpotAngels, Parkopedia, EasyPark, CellOPark), AU parking-fine appeal service pricing, freemium-to-paid conversion rates, usage-metered free tiers, one-off versus subscription for low-frequency products, and AUD price points

The headline finding is uncomfortable: the comparable set for ParkProof's paid tier is almost empty, and the one company that had the best consumer parking data in the world just quit the consumer app business. Parkopedia's standalone consumer app carries an in-store notice that it will no longer be supported from 1 July 2026, and the company now sells to automakers. SpotAngels is the only real consumer parking subscription benchmark left at USD 4.99/month or 29.99/year, and notably it put co-driver car sharing behind that paywall in 2025, which independently validates ParkProof's family sharing idea as a paid feature. Meanwhile Australia already has a live, competitive, one-off-priced AI parking-fine-appeal market: FineAppeal.com.au charges A$9.99 for a parking appeal, Fight My Fine charges A$10, Fine Dodger starts around A$9.99, and every one of them advertises "no subscriptions" and offers a free case-strength check before payment. That anchor is set and ParkProof cannot price a Dispute Pack above it without a reason. On conversion, the honest number is bleak: RevenueCat's 2026 study of 115,000+ apps puts median freemium download-to-paid at 2.1% by day 35 versus 10.7% for a hard paywall, with 8x the revenue per install and essentially identical year-one retention. Utilities as a category actually converts better than average on trials (6.5% median download-to-trial at day 30) and retains well at first renewal (58.1% per Adapty), but there is no published conversion benchmark anywhere for usage-metered free tiers, so a "10 scans a month" limit is an untested bet dressed up as a best practice. The genuinely open ground: nobody in the market sells prevention, nobody sells evidence captured before the fine, and nobody except CellOPark bills honestly for a product you only use a few times a year.

This is the single most directly comparable product to ParkProof's planned paid tier, and it independently validates family car sharing as a paid feature rather than a free one.

- **Parkopedia has exited the standalone consumer app and gone B2B automotive** _(confidence: medium)_  
  The Apple App Store listing for Parkopedia Parking carries a 'We're Moving' banner stating the app will no longer be supported from 1 July 2026. A third-party tracker lists the iOS app as removed from the App Store. Parkopedia is now positioned as a parking and EV charging data provider to automakers, with investment from Volkswagen Group. The company with arguably the best global parking dataset could not make a consumer app pay. That is a signal worth taking seriously, not a footnote.  
  Source: <https://apps.apple.com/app/id409340361>

- **Fight My Fine charges a flat A$10 per parking or toll letter with a conditional full refund, and markets 'no percentage of your fine' as a virtue** _(confidence: high)_

If grounds are rated Strong and the authority rejects the dispute in full, the fee is fully refunded on upload of the written decision within 30 days. Includes a camera-photo check and up to 3 regenerations per session. Launch offer of 50% off the first letter for the first 25 customers.

- **A third AU player, Fine Dodger, starts from A$9.99 with a money-back guarantee, and Parksy's appeal letter generator is free** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Fine Dodger advertises pricing from A$9.99 with a money-back guarantee on qualifying case scores, though its structured data lists 'From $5.49', so the advertised floor is inconsistent. Parksy offers a free AI appeal letter generator for Australian infringements. Fine Dodger's own page also points out that internal reviews in NSW are free regardless of whether you use a service. The City of Melbourne internal review route is also free. So the paid-letter market is competing against a free alternative that councils provide, plus at least one free AI tool.

- **Median freemium download-to-paid conversion is 2.1% by day 35, versus 10.7% for a hard paywall, with 8x the revenue per install and no retention penalty** _(confidence: high)_  
  RevenueCat's State of Subscription Apps 2026, covering 115,000+ apps and over $16B in revenue, reports median day-35 download-to-paid of 10.7% for hard paywalls (top quartile above 20%) versus 2.1% for freemium (top quartile above 4.5%). Day-60 revenue per install is $3.09 for hard paywall versus $0.38 for freemium. Critically, one-year retention of yearly subscribers is 28% freemium versus 27% hard paywall, a difference RevenueCat calls statistically negligible. Freemium conversion was flat year on year; hard paywall conversion fell from 12.1% in 2025. Caveat: RevenueCat is a subscription billing vendor with a commercial interest in this framing.  
  Source: <https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026>
- **Utilities is one of the better-converting categories on trials, with 6.5% median download-to-trial at day 30** _(confidence: medium)_  
  RevenueCat's 2026 report lists Utilities among the higher performers for day-30 download-to-trial conversion at 6.5% median. It notes trial-to-paid variance is wide for Utilities and places the category in the middle tier for month-1 realised LTV, but publishes no numeric value for either. No Utilities figures appear for monetisation mix, plan mix or revenue per install.  
  Source: <https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps>
- **Utility apps lead first-renewal retention at 58.1% and produce the highest trial-user LTV at $68.90 over 12 months** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Adapty's State of In-App Subscriptions reports Utilities with the highest weekly subscription revenue share (73.6%), the highest 12-month trial-user LTV ($68.90), and the best first-renewal retention (58.1%). - **Annual beats monthly roughly 3x on LTV from billing period alone, but annual churn has got sharply worse** _(confidence: medium)_

Both can be true if the denominators differ (retention of cohorts that renewed versus cancellation intent registered during the term), but the direction of travel is that annual plans are no longer the safe harbour they were.  
  Source: <https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026>
- **About 36.5% of apps now sell something other than a subscription, and non-subscription plan share grew from 6.4% to 10.3% in two years** _(confidence: medium)_  
  RevenueCat 2026 monetisation mix: subscriptions only 63.5%, subscriptions plus lifetime 23.2%, subscriptions plus consumables 10.7%, all three 2.5%. That implies roughly 26% of apps offer a lifetime option and about 13% offer consumables. Adapty's 2026 data puts one-time, consumable and lifetime plans at 10.3% of monetisation models in 2025, up from 6.4% in 2023. RevenueCat's 2025 report said 35% of apps mix subscriptions with consumables or lifetime purchases. Hybrid is now mainstream, not exotic.  
  Source: <https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps>
- **Lifetime-dominant apps earn the LOWEST revenue per install of any plan type: $0.24 at day 60 versus $0.46 for yearly-dominant** _(confidence: medium)_  
  RevenueCat 2026 day-14/day-60 revenue per install by dominant plan type: yearly-dominant $0.36/$0.46, weekly-dominant $0.19/$0.32, monthly-dominant $0.18/$0.29, lifetime-dominant $0.19/$0.24. This classifies apps by their most-sold plan, not strictly by model. Airbridge separately warns that the main downside of a lifetime offer is cannibalisation: your most loyal, highest-retention users are the ones most likely to buy it and then stop paying. This is the strongest counter-argument to making a one-off Dispute Pack the primary monetisation rather than an add-on.  
  Source: <https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps>
- **Apple keeps 30% of one-off in-app purchases forever. Subscriptions drop to 15% after year one. One-off pricing is structurally worse on take rate inside an app store** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Per Apple's developer documentation as summarised by Airbridge, in-app purchase commission is 30%, subscriptions fall to 15% after a subscriber's first year, and one-time purchases stay at 30% indefinitely. Airbridge's worked example: a $4.99 one-off nets $3.49 per payer after Apple's cut and needs a 60%+ conversion rate to cover a $5 cost per install, whereas a $9.99/month subscription nets about $28 over four months and breaks even around months 2 to 3. Airbridge also notes Apple's guidelines bar removing functionality existing purchasers already paid for, which constrains any later move from one-off to subscription.  
  Source: <https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/subscription-vs-one-time-purchase-app>
- **The practitioner consensus is that bounded, event-driven problems should be priced one-off, and willingness to pay tracks urgency and consequence rather than frequency** _(confidence: medium)_  
  Airbridge's framing: 'does your app deliver value continuously, or does it solve a bounded problem? Subscriptions work for continuous value. One-time purchases work for bounded value.' It names tax calculators, resume builders and photo enhancers as cases where subscriptions create friction because users resent paying in months they do not use the app. Observed one-off price ranges it reports (unattributed): utility apps such as scanners and converters $2.99 to $9.99, photo and video editing $4.99 to $29.99. It also notes that a weather app used five times a day can have low willingness to pay, while a rarely-used tool with high stakes can command a lot. That last point is the argument for ParkProof's Dispute Pack.  
  Source: <https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/subscription-vs-one-time-purchase-app>
- **There is no published conversion benchmark anywhere for usage-metered free tiers in consumer apps. Every quota recommendation in the market is an unmeasured heuristic** _(confidence: low)_  
  I searched specifically for scans-per-month or credits-per-month free-tier conversion data and found none. What exists is advice: gate after 3 to 5 actions for AI tools (x1.new, no data behind it), and set the limit so roughly 20 to 30% of active free accounts hit it within 30 days, positioned above the point where users first find value (SaasDash, a vendor blog, no measured basis). Superwall's guidance uses a coin-scanning app as its illustration and suggests prompting after five scans with a warning as users approach the limit, again as advice not results. Marking this honestly as a gap: a '10 uses a month' free tier is a reasonable design but it is not an evidence-backed one, and ParkProof will be generating the benchmark rather than following it.  
  Source: <https://saasdash.ai/blog/free-to-paid-metering-threshold-tuning>
- **Consumer utility apps (weather, scanner, calculator) sit in the lowest free-to-paid band of any category at 1 to 3%, and ad removal is the usual lever** _(confidence: low)_

It cites RevenueCat and OpenView but does not show the underlying cut. Entertainment is cited at 1 to 3% and productivity/business at 7 to 10%. Treat the exact numbers as directional: this is a secondary blog, not a primary dataset. - **Family tiers in consumer subscriptions price at roughly 1.55x to 1.7x the individual price** _(confidence: low)_  
  Apple Music individual $10.99 versus family $16.99 is about a 55% uplift. YouTube Premium's 2026 increase took individual to $15.99 and family to $26.99, about 69% (sources differ slightly on the exact figures). No published benchmark exists for family or multi-user pricing in utility apps specifically, so this is an analogue from streaming rather than a category figure. The practical read for ParkProof: a household tier at 1.5x to 1.7x individual is the market-normal shape, and SpotAngels has already demonstrated an operator willing to put co-driver sharing behind a paywall.  
  Source: <https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/?p=223751>
- **The success-fee model for parking tickets has been tried and died: Fixed charged 35% of the fine avoided and admitted only 20 to 30% of tickets were beaten** _(confidence: low)_  
  TechCrunch coverage of Fixed describes a flat $1.95 card-processing charge plus a success fee of 35% of the fine avoided (an earlier article cited 25%), and reports that only around 20 to 30% of tickets Fixed received were actually beaten. The company no longer operates. Notably, Fight My Fine now markets 'no percentage of your fine' as an explicit selling point, and Beat It in the UK uses a small subscription (£3 per 3 months per vehicle) plus a £3 win fee only on full cancellation. The percentage-of-savings model appears to be a solved question with a negative answer. Confidence is medium because the TechCrunch URLs returned by search are in a non-canonical form and the articles are old.

#### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody in parking sells prevention. Every monetised product in this market is either payment convenience (EasyPark, CellOPark, PayStay take a cut of a transaction you were already going to make) or post-hoc dispute (FineAppeal, Fight My Fine, Fine Dodger charge after the fine already exists). Not one company charges money for 'do not get the fine in the first place'. ParkProof is the only product positioned there and it has no competitor to price against, which is both the opportunity and the reason there is no benchmark.
- The Australian dispute market sells a letter, not evidence. Every AU appeal service generates persuasive text from what the user types into a form after the fact. None of them hold a time-stamped, GPS-stamped, cryptographically signed record captured at the moment of parking. ParkProof's KMS-signed evidence chain is the only genuinely defensible asset in this entire competitive set, and literally nobody in the market prices it or even markets the concept. Right now ParkProof is planning to sell the same commodity letter as everyone else at the same A$9.99.
- Nobody has published conversion data for usage-metered free tiers in consumer apps. Not RevenueCat across 115,000 apps, not Adapty, not Airbridge. Every '3 to 5 free uses' or '20 to 30% of users should hit the limit' recommendation in the market is a vendor heuristic with no measured basis behind it. This cuts both ways: ParkProof cannot copy a proven number, and whatever it learns is genuinely novel.
- Nobody solves the low-frequency billing mismatch honestly. CellOPark's 'charged only in months you use the app' at A$1.99 per vehicle is the single closest thing to usage-honest consumer billing found anywhere in this research, and it is almost unknown. Every monetisation source names 'charging monthly for a twice-a-year job' as the core failure mode for low-frequency utilities, and the market's answer is to push annual plans and absorb the churn (now 72% of annual subscribers cancelling within year one). There is no good product in the market for someone who parks in a paid bay eleven times a year.
- Nobody in parking has a credible household or shared-car product. SpotAngels put co-driver sharing behind Plus in 2025 and that is the entire competitive field globally. No Australian player offers it at all. Shared-car households (one car, two drivers, both get fined) are a real and completely unserved segment, and it is the one feature with a demonstrated operator willing to charge for it.
- No AUD-specific pricing benchmark exists for consumer utility apps. The published regional buckets stop at North America, Western Europe, Asia-Pacific and IN/SEA. Australia is priced by converting a US tier and hoping. The best available local anchors are Weatherzone at A$5.99/month and WillyWeather at around A$4/month, both weather apps, both with brand recognition ParkProof does not yet have.
- Nobody bundles the before and the after. The dispute services have no idea whether you were legitimately parked, so their letters are argument without proof. The parking payment apps have no dispute path at all. A single product that captures evidence pre-emptively and then converts that evidence into an appeal is structurally unoccupied, and it is the only version of the Dispute Pack that can justify pricing above the A$9.99 commodity anchor.
- Pre-2027 compliance is unaddressed and currently sellable. Nothing in this research suggests any AU consumer app is visibly building for the 1 July 2027 subscription rules. With 75% of Australians reporting a bad cancellation experience and 94% rating flexibility to cancel as a top benefit, one-tap cancellation and honest renewal notices are a marketing claim today and table stakes in 20 months. Almost nobody is making that claim.

#### Feature ideas

- **Price the Dispute Pack at A$9.99 one-off, and make the signed evidence record the reason it is worth that, not the letter**  
  The anchor is already set by three live Australian competitors and there is no room to price above it on letter quality alone. But ParkProof is the only one that can attach a tamper-evident, time-stamped, GPS-stamped, KMS-signed record captured before the fine existed. Sell the pack as 'proof plus letter' rather than 'letter'. That is the only story that survives a price comparison against a competitor charging the same A$9.99 and a council offering internal review for free.  
  _Evidence:_ FineAppeal.com.au charges A$9.99 for a parking appeal with 75+ councils and 15 legal grounds per state. Fight My Fine charges A$10 flat. Fine Dodger starts from A$9.99. All three advertise one-off pricing with no subscription. None of them hold evidence captured at the time of parking. Melbourne fines run A$96 to A$204, so A$9.99 is about 5 to 10% of the amount at risk.
- **Give a free case-strength assessment before payment, and refund in full if you rate the case strong and the appeal fails**  
  Both verified Australian competitors already do this. Not doing it makes ParkProof read as less confident than the incumbents at the same price. It also converts the free tier into a qualification funnel rather than a usage cap, which sidesteps the fact that there is no evidence metered caps convert.  
  _Evidence:_ Fight My Fine offers a free 60-second grounds check with no sign-up, a free strong/moderate/limited rating before payment, and a full refund if grounds are rated Strong and the authority rejects in full (written decision uploaded within 30 days). FineAppeal.com.au offers a free AI assessment before payment and states it will not take your money if the grounds are not there.
- **Do not make a metered free tier the primary paywall. Keep scanning free and put a long trial in front of the subscription instead**  
  Freemium is the worst-converting access model in the dataset and consumer utilities are the worst-converting category within it. A metered cap is additionally unproven. A long trial with an opt-in (no card) start converts an order of magnitude better and fits a product whose value needs several real parking events to demonstrate.  
  _Evidence:_ RevenueCat 2026: freemium median 2.1% download-to-paid at day 35 versus 10.7% hard paywall, with day-60 RPI of $0.38 versus $3.09 and statistically identical year-one retention. Adapty: freemium trial-to-paid 2.6% versus 18.2% for a no-card opt-in trial. RevenueCat: trials of 17 to 32 days convert at median 42.5% versus 25.5% for trials under four days. Consumer utilities sit at 1 to 3% free-to-paid per Bolder Apps. No published benchmark exists for metered caps at all.
- **If a subscription ships, price annual-first at A$29.99 to A$39.99 per year and de-emphasise monthly**  
  A$29.99 is the single most common annual price globally and the median sits at A$34.80, so it reads as normal rather than cheap or greedy. Annual also solves part of the low-frequency mismatch: a driver who parks eleven times a year can rationalise $30 once, not $5 twelve times.  
  _Evidence:_ RevenueCat 2026: yearly most common $30, median risen to $34.80, category medians $20 to $44.99. Airbridge: annual subscribers retain 44.1% at 12 months versus 17.5% monthly and 3.4% weekly, roughly a 3x LTV gap from billing period alone. Caveat worth pre-deciding: RevenueCat 2026 also reports 72% of annual subscribers cancelling within year one, up from 56%, with 35% of those cancellations in month one, so the renewal notice and the first-month experience matter more than the price.
- **Set monthly at A$4.99 to A$5.99, not A$9.99**  
  The temptation is to anchor on the $9.99 'most common' monthly price, but that number sits in the top quartile of the global distribution and it is set by apps with brands. The relevant local comparison is the Australian weather incumbents, and ParkProof cannot charge more than they do.  
  _Evidence:_ Weatherzone Pro is AUD $5.99 per month per DTN's own help centre. WillyWeather+ is listed around $4.00 monthly / $36.00 yearly. SpotAngels Plus is USD $4.99 per month. Airbridge notes the most common monthly price of $9.99 sits in the top quartile of the distribution, not the middle, with a median of $6.68.
- **Charge only in months the app is actually used**  
  This is the strongest single idea in the research and it comes from an Australian competitor almost nobody talks about. It directly answers the objection that kills low-frequency utility subscriptions, it is a genuine trust differentiator in a market where 75% of Australians have had a bad cancellation experience, and it pre-empts the 2027 reforms by making the pricing self-evidently fair. It also converts cancellation pressure into dormancy rather than churn.  
  _Evidence:_ CellOPark Australia's App Store listing states Premium is $1.99 per vehicle per month and is only charged in months the app is used. Every monetisation source names the billing-frequency mismatch as the core failure mode for infrequently-used utilities: Airbridge ('users resent paying in months they do not use the app'), RevenueCat's low-intent playbook, and the 72% annual cancellation figure. Price confidence is medium: I could not verify the CellOPark figure against the company's own site.
- **Sell credits or packs for the expensive AI actions rather than metering the cheap ones**  
  Scanning a sign is the habit-forming action and should stay unlimited and free. Drafting an appeal, generating a signed evidence export, or producing a Dispute Pack are the expensive, high-intent, high-stakes actions and they are what people will actually pay for at the moment of need. This is also what RevenueCat itself recommends for the 90% of installs that will never subscribe.  
  _Evidence:_ RevenueCat's low-intent monetisation guide leads with consumables/credits and one-time unlocks, naming Weather on the Way's one-time lifetime unlock as an example. RevenueCat 2026 mix data: 10.7% of apps already pair subscriptions with consumables, 23.2% with lifetime, 36.5% sell some non-subscription purchase. Adapty: non-subscription plan share rose from 6.4% in 2023 to 10.3% in 2025. Airbridge puts one-off utility pricing at $2.99 to $9.99.
- **Price the family / shared-car tier at 1.5x to 1.7x individual, and treat it as the main subscription upgrade rather than a side feature**  
  It is the one paid feature with a demonstrated operator behind it, it has essentially zero competition globally and none in Australia, and it is the only part of ParkProof that is genuinely continuous-value rather than event-driven. A shared car in a two-driver household generates parking events far more often than a single driver does, which is exactly the frequency profile a subscription needs.  
  _Evidence:_ SpotAngels moved 'sharing a car with a co-driver' into Plus (USD $4.99/month, $29.99/year) from 15 May 2025. Apple Music family prices at about 55% above individual, YouTube Premium at about 69%. No parking app in Australia offers shared-car functionality at any price.
- **Monetise through Stripe on the web, not through an app store, and treat that as a deliberate pricing advantage**  
  ParkProof is already a PWA, which means it can keep roughly 97% of a A$9.99 one-off instead of 70%. That is the difference between a one-off pack being marginal and being viable. It is also the single biggest structural reason ParkProof can price at the A$9.99 market anchor and still make money where an App Store competitor cannot.  
  _Evidence:_ Apple's commission is 30% on in-app purchases, dropping to 15% only for subscriptions after a subscriber's first year, with one-time purchases staying at 30% indefinitely. Airbridge's worked example: a $4.99 one-off nets $3.49 after Apple's cut. Apple also prohibits removing functionality existing purchasers have already paid for, which constrains later model changes.
- **Build the 1 July 2027 subscription compliance into the first paid release and market it now**  
  Upfront disclosure, prescribed renewal notices and one-tap online cancellation are going to be legally required in 20 months. Building them later means retrofitting a billing system under deadline. Building them now costs almost nothing and is a trust claim in a market that is visibly sick of cancellation friction. For a portfolio piece, it is also a demonstrably PM-shaped decision: regulatory horizon-scanning turned into a product requirement.  
  _Evidence:_ From 1 July 2027 Australia requires clear upfront subscription disclosure, prescribed information during the subscription, cancellation methods that are easy to find and limited to reasonably necessary steps (with online cancellation required in certain cases), and consistent messaging across advertising, checkout and terms, plus a separate prohibition on dark patterns applying to conduct from that date regardless of contract date. CPRC 2024: three in four Australians have had a negative cancellation experience. BSA Nov 2025: 94 to 95% of Australians rate ease of cancellation among the top benefits of subscriptions.
- **Rule out any percentage-of-fine or success-fee pricing now so it never comes up again**  
  It looks elegant on a whiteboard (no win, no fee) and it has already been tried and buried. It also requires ParkProof to verify outcomes, which means chasing users for council decision letters, which is operationally miserable for a solo developer. Pre-deciding this removes a recurring temptation.  
  _Evidence:_ Fixed charged a 35% success fee on the fine avoided (25% in an earlier version) plus a $1.95 processing charge, reported only 20 to 30% of tickets were actually beaten, and no longer operates. Fight My Fine now markets 'no subscriptions, no percentage of your fine' as an explicit selling point, which indicates the model is a known negative in the category.
- **Instrument the metered-limit experiment properly, because the resulting data is genuinely novel**  
  There is no published benchmark for quota-based free tiers in consumer apps anywhere. If ParkProof ships a usage limit and measures how many free users hit it, at what quota, and what fraction then convert, that is a finding nobody in the market has published. For a portfolio piece aimed at hiring PMs, 'I generated the benchmark because none existed' is a far better story than 'I copied a best practice'.  
  _Evidence:_ Searches across RevenueCat (115,000+ apps), Adapty and Airbridge returned no scans-per-month or credits-per-month conversion data. The only available guidance is unmeasured: gate after 3 to 5 actions (x1.new), target 20 to 30% of active free accounts hitting the limit within 30 days (SaasDash), and warn users as they approach the cap (Superwall). All three are vendor blogs with no data behind the numbers.

### Report 7: Emerging and adjacent needs an Australian consumer parking app could serve (EV bays, accessible permits, digital residential permits, event clearways, school zones, loading zones, street sweeping, plus 2025-26 regulatory and technology change)

The honest headline: EV charging bays are the loudest emerging story but the weakest fine opportunity, while accessible permits and street sweeping are quieter and far richer. EV "ICEing" penalties are spectacular on paper (up to $3,200 in the ACT) and almost never issued in practice (466 fines across NSW in 15 months; zero in ACT-patrolled car parks), so the real EV need is not the fine, it is the brand-new dual-clock problem where a council's signed time limit and an operator's $1-per-minute idle fee run on different starts, different grace periods and different caps. Accessible parking is the single most under-served area: the "nationally harmonised" scheme hides material differences (Victoria gives double time on ordinary bays, NSW gives free metered parking instead and no double time, Whitehorse and Tasmania require you to pay the maximum period first before double time begins, and the permit never authorises a loading zone) and no app I found computes any of it. Street sweeping is a genuine Australian restriction in inner-Melbourne councils, it recurs weekly, resident permits do not exempt you, and the day differs by side of street, which makes it the most calendar-shaped parking rule in the country and nobody turns it into a reminder. The volume data is deflating for exotic-sign features: Wollongong's own report shows 97% of all council fines are parking and the top offence is failing to pay or obey a sign, and Merri-bek's $12M refund scandal ranked overstaying a time limit first, so timers and reminders are where the money is. The biggest regulatory shift is NSW's Fines Amendment (Parking Fines) Act 2024 and Regulation 2025, in force since 1 July 2025: a physical notice is now mandatory, councils must hand photographic evidence to Revenue NSW, and all 128 councils must publish their fine data, which creates both a structural invalidity ground and a public dataset nobody is using consumer-side. Victoria has the strongest precedent for structural invalidity, with 268,000-plus fines put in doubt by the Ombudsman and another 250,000 overcharged by Merri-bek since 2013 because no council resolution authorised the rate. Finally, a warning: the read-the-sign layer is now commoditised, with at least six AI sign readers serving Australia in 2026, two of them openly Claude-powered, so differentiation has to sit in what happens after the read.

#### Findings

- **EV charging bay penalties vary by more than 40x across Australia with no national rule, no standard time limit and no single signage standard** _(confidence: medium)_

The NTC's 14th amendment package (Jan 2023) proposed separate signs for 'charging' versus 'non-charging' EV parking and electric-motorbike-only bays, adopted unevenly by states. A driver cannot know the rule from the sign alone.

  Transport for NSW reported 466 fines for incorrectly parking in EV charging spaces between January 2023 and March 2024, totalling $58,953. The Victorian Double Time permit (formerly green) allows only ordinary bays at twice the limit and is Victoria-only. Permanent permits now run 5 years, up from 3; temporary permits come in 6, 12 or 24 months; occupational therapists can now assess alongside GPs. Administered by all 79 Victorian councils, with the permit types and Double Time eligibility set out in Victoria Government Gazette S 737, 30 December 2024.

  Whitehorse states you must pay for the initial time period before you can stay for double it, so paying one hour in a 1P ticketed bay buys a second free hour. Tasmania's Transport department says the same: the permit gives twice the signed time where the limit is one hour or longer, but you must first have paid for the maximum parking time before the extra time begins. This is exactly the kind of conditional arithmetic a sign cannot express and a human gets wrong.

In WA, the double-time concession I found is a City of Perth policy, not a statewide rule.

  City of Melbourne, 2018-19: about 20,000 internal review requests against roughly 286,000 infringements (7%), with about 22.5% of reviewed infringements withdrawn (4,319). #### Gaps nobody fills

- Nobody turns a permit into a computed answer. Every sign reader answers for a permitless generic driver. No app I found asks 'do you hold an Australian Disability Parking Permit, a Victorian Double Time permit, an ACROD permit, a NSW MPS permit, or a resident permit in this council', and then changes the leave-by time accordingly, including the conditional 'you must pay the maximum signed period first before double time starts' rule in Whitehorse and Tasmania, and the NSW case where the concession is free metered parking rather than extra time.
- Nobody models the vehicle. Loading zone eligibility turns on whether the vehicle is a goods vehicle and, in Victoria, on whether a business name or 'courier' is permanently displayed. EV bay legality turns on whether the car is an EV or externally-rechargeable PHEV and whether it is actively plugged in and charging. Motorbike bays are their own category. No sign reader asks what you drive, so every answer it gives on those three sign types is a guess.
- Nobody reconciles the two clocks at an EV charging bay. The council's signed parking time limit and the operator's idle fee start at different moments (end of charge versus arrival), have different grace periods (0, 10 or 15 minutes depending on council) and different caps ($204 at Wyndham, uncapped elsewhere). A driver standing at a Glen Eira charger cannot work out their real deadline from anything in front of them.
- Nobody treats a recurring restriction as a calendar entry. Street sweeping in inner-Melbourne councils is a weekly 1-2 hour No Parking, on a different day for each side of the street, which resident permits do not exempt. It is the single most calendar-shaped parking rule in Australia and the exemption process is a phone call with two weeks notice. No app ingests a sweeping sign and produces a recurring reminder.
- Nobody checks the administrative validity of a fine. Every appeal tool writes a letter about circumstances. None checks the structural grounds that have actually voided hundreds of thousands of Australian fines: was a physical notice attached (mandatory in NSW since 1 July 2025), did the issuing council have a resolution authorising that penalty amount on that date (the Merri-bek defect), was the internal review outsourced to a contractor (the Victorian Ombudsman defect), and is the issuing council currently running a refund scheme.
- Nobody surfaces the newly-mandated council fine data back to drivers. All 128 NSW councils must now publish notices issued, notices withdrawn and the grounds for not attaching a notice, on a fixed schedule, and Revenue NSW publishes offence datasets. No consumer-facing product uses any of it, so no driver can see the withdrawal rate of the council about to fine them.
- There is no machine-readable feed of special-event or temporary parking restrictions anywhere in Australia that I could find. Temporary signs legally override permanent ones, towing is the enforcement mechanism, and the only notice is a physical sign posted days in advance. Every council I checked publishes this as prose or PDFs, or not at all.
- Nobody handles the digital-permit activation failure mode. As councils tie permits to registrations with activated sessions, capped free allowances and per-day charges, a fully entitled resident can be fined for an administrative omission. Northern Beaches alone is migrating 250,000+ permits into this shape, and the council's own response was to make the mechanics easier rather than remove them.
- Nobody tells you which payment app or method applies at this specific kerb. Brisbane runs CellOPark and PayStay, Cairns uses EasyPark, Willoughby uses Park'nPay, North Sydney uses PayStay plus Touch N Go. A driver crossing council boundaries has no way to know what they will need before they arrive, and over a quarter of Brisbane's metered spaces will not take any physical payment at all.
- Nobody serves the digitally-excluded driver that councils are now creating. Cashless and app-only kerbs are expanding with explicit public criticism from COTA Australia and councillors, and the stated harms (forgotten passwords, inaccessible email, QR-code distrust) are account and onboarding problems, not parking problems. An anonymous-by-default product is unusually well placed here and nothing addresses it.

#### Feature ideas

- **Permit-aware answers: ask once which permit the user holds, then change the computed leave-by time, including the pay-first-then-double-time conditional**  
  Tier 1. This is the highest-value finding in the whole brief and it is a direct extension of the compute ParkProof already does, not a new product. The permit changes the arithmetic, not the sign reading, so it slots into the existing 'compute leave-by per rule, take the earliest' logic. It also moves the app from 'nice utility' to 'tool a permit holder depends on', which is a retention shape ParkProof currently lacks, and it serves a user group for whom getting it wrong costs $581 in NSW. Start with Victoria's two permit types since that is the home market and the gazetted rules are clear, then NSW.  
  _Evidence:_ Victoria has two distinct permits with different rights, one not recognised interstate (Whitehorse council, Victoria Government Gazette S 737, 30 Dec 2024). Whitehorse and Tasmania both require payment for the maximum signed period before double time begins. NSW grants free metered parking instead of double time and explicitly warns concessions vary between states. WA's double time is a City of Perth policy, not statewide. No app found does any of this.
- **Vehicle profile: a one-time 'what do you drive' answer (car, ute or van with permanent business signage, EV or plug-in hybrid, motorbike) that changes loading-zone and EV-bay verdicts**  
  Tier 1 and the cheapest high-value item on this list. It is one onboarding question and a conditional branch in the prompt, and it fixes a class of answer that every competitor currently gets wrong by assumption. It also pairs with the permit work so both land as one release: the app stops answering for a generic driver and starts answering for this driver in this vehicle. That is a defensible positioning line against the six commoditised sign readers.  
  _Evidence:_ Victorian loading zones require a business name or 'courier' permanently displayed, with temporary roof or window signs explicitly not acceptable; NSW includes utes and station wagons as goods vehicles; Queensland distinguishes 2 minutes for passengers from 20 minutes for goods; Adelaide's definition excludes station wagons. EV bays require the vehicle to be an EV and actively charging. No sign reader found asks what the user drives.
- **Street sweeping recurring reminder: scan the sweeping sign once, get a repeating calendar entry for the correct side of the street**  
  Tier 1 for the Melbourne market, and it reuses machinery that already exists. ParkProof already generates multi-VALARM .ics files, so the build is a sign-pattern recogniser plus a recurrence rule. The product logic is unusually clean: a weekly restriction that a resident permit does not exempt, differs by side of street, and whose only existing mitigation is phoning a council two weeks in advance. It also converts ParkProof from an occasional scan-when-unsure tool into something with a weekly reason to exist, which is the retention gap the portfolio notes already flag.  
  _Evidence:_ Stonnington: No Parking 1 to 2 hours a week, applies weekly even where sweeping is fortnightly, opposite sides of the street restricted on different days, and 'resident parking permits do not exempt your vehicle from street sweeping restrictions'. Exemptions require phoning the Stonnington Parking Alliance, two weeks ahead for extended exemptions, and are void if the car is seen moving. Port Phillip sweeps residential streets four-weekly with published schedules.
- **Rebuild the planned ticket-validity checker around structural grounds rather than sympathetic circumstances**  
  Tier 1 and a sharpening of something already on the roadmap. Every appeal tool in the market argues circumstances. The grounds that have actually voided hundreds of thousands of Australian fines are administrative and checkable from a photo of the notice plus the issuing council and date: was a physical notice attached, does the penalty amount match the authorised rate for that date, is this council in a known refund scheme, was the internal review outsourced in the relevant window. That is a deterministic rules engine, not a language model guess, which makes it both cheaper to run and far more credible. Only 7% of drivers ever request a review, so the funnel is wide open at the top.  
  _Evidence:_ NSW requires a physical notice since 1 July 2025 and Revenue NSW can invalidate non-compliant fines across all 128 councils. Merri-bek overcharged up to 250,000 fines since 2013 because no resolution authorised the rate, refunding $10.7M-$14.7M at $43-$59 per fine. The Victorian Ombudsman put 268,000+ fines in doubt across 12 councils and agencies with about $19.5M refunded. Yarra Ranges had wrong amounts on 4 offence types from 2005 to 2025. City of Melbourne 2018-19: 7% of drivers sought review, about 22.5% succeeded.
- **EV dual-clock timer: two countdowns at a charging bay, one for the signed parking limit and one for the operator or council idle fee with its own grace period and cap**  
  Tier 2. The need is real and genuinely unserved, but the EV driver population is still small and the fine volume is negligible, so this earns its place on confusion rather than on fines. Keep the scope narrow: do not try to model every charging network's tariff, just capture the idle fee terms from the signage or the user and run the second clock. It is a strong portfolio demonstration of 'read the signal, not the headline' because it deliberately ignores the dramatic $3,200 ICEing penalty that nobody is actually issuing.  
  _Evidence:_ Glen Eira $1 per minute past the advertised parking time from 1 October 2026 plus 25c/kWh; Wyndham $1 per minute with a 15-minute post-charge grace and a $204 cap; Canterbury-Bankstown $1 per minute only after two hours with a 10-minute grace. Three councils, three trigger points. Against that: only 466 ICEing fines across NSW in 15 months, and zero in ACT-patrolled car parks.
- **Digital permit activation checklist: a per-session reminder that the right registration is activated for the right day, with the remaining free-session count**  
  Tier 2 to 3, and worth deferring until more councils have migrated. The failure mode is new, specific and will grow, but today it is concentrated in one Sydney council's permit scheme, which is a narrow wedge for a Melbourne-based product. Revisit when a Victorian council announces a comparable migration. The reason to note it now is that it is a reminder-shaped problem, which means it falls out almost free once the street-sweeping recurrence work exists.  
  _Evidence:_ Northern Beaches is migrating 250,000+ permits across 7 schemes under a $474k contract, staged Nov 2025 to mid-2026, with permits tied to registrations. Manly multi-use permits now need per-use online registration up to 7 days ahead, 24-hour sessions, 40 free sessions a year then $5 a day, and about $120 fine if unactivated. Residents called it 'impossible'. City of Melbourne has already moved to online-only permits with no paper option.
- **School drop-off two-minute timer with a crossing-proximity warning**  
  Tier 3. The rule is genuinely confusing and the zero-tolerance enforcement posture is real, but the evidence that it generates parking fine volume is weak, and the one quoted statistic (24% of NSW parking fines) could not be traced to any Transport for NSW source. The heavy school-zone money is in speed cameras, which is outside the product. Build it only if the sweeping and permit work lands and you want a second recurring daily-habit hook.  
  _Evidence:_ Kiss and Go zones carry a roughly two-minute limit; parking within 20 metres before or 10 metres after a school crossing is illegal even outside school zone hours; Knox City Council states a 'zero-tolerance approach to parking offences around school areas, including brief illegal stops'. The 24% claim appears only on consumer sites with no traceable source, so treat it as a gap. One Mosman school-zone speed camera took $933,634 from 4,349 drivers in twelve months, which is speeding, not parking.
- **Event and temporary sign warning: flag when a scanned sign is a temporary or event overlay and escalate the consequence from fine to tow**  
  Tier 3, and be honest that there is no data feed to automate it. The value is in the consequence framing rather than detection: a driver who misreads a temporary clearway does not get a $209 fine, they get a $504.70 release fee that becomes $984.68 after fourteen days, and the NSW 10-minute grace period explicitly does not apply. That is a different decision for the user. Implement as prompt-level recognition of temporary signage plus a consequence warning, not as a restriction database.  
  _Evidence:_ The Barossa Council states that event and roadworks signs override permanent signage. City of Melbourne as at 1 July 2026: clearway or tow-away release fee $504.70 plus $209 fine, rising to $984.68 after 14 days in the overflow yard. The NSW grace period excludes clearways and special-event parking. No machine-readable feed of temporary restrictions was found anywhere in Australia.
- **Council profile card: show the issuing council's own published fine and withdrawal data alongside a scan or a fine**  
  Tier 3 and genuinely novel, but it is a differentiator rather than a need. NSW has just forced 128 councils to publish exactly the data that would let a driver judge their odds, and nobody consumer-side is reading it. The honest constraint is that it is NSW-only, and ParkProof's home market is Victoria, where no equivalent mandate exists. Park it as a monetisation-tier sweetener for the dispute product rather than a standalone build.  
  _Evidence:_ Fines Amendment (Parking Fines) Regulation 2025 requires NSW councils to publish notices issued, notices withdrawn and the grounds for not attaching a notice, quarterly then half-yearly within 28 days of period end. City of Sydney published its Jan-Jun 2026 report on 30 July 2026. Revenue NSW publishes a Parking offences dataset. Mosman, North Sydney and Willoughby issued 123,095 parking tickets worth $23.7M in the twelve months to June 2026.
